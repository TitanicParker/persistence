# Pixels of Clarity — Book Plan

> **Status:** working architecture for the ten-year-old pedagogical edition of Persistence.
>
> **Governing commitment:** Do not simplify away the structure. Simplify the route into it.

## What this book is for

This book is a route into **Persistence / the Constraint Grammar of Completed Intelligibility** for roughly ten-year-old readers, teachers, parents, and other adults learning alongside them.

The success condition is not that a child can recite a taxonomy. It is that a child comes away with a clear, usable mental picture of:

- completed intelligibility;
- the 6 Transformation Patterns;
- the 3 Completion Topologies;
- the 3 Persistence Modes;
- the difference between those three dimensions;
- Dimensional Conjunction;
- the 54-position grammar;
- neighbouring-cell discrimination;
- the possibility of **no coordinate** where no completion has occurred;
- the difference between intelligibility and truth;
- how to notice and discuss these structures in ordinary language and life.

The terminal competence is:

> **I can notice a completion, identify its structural form, locate it in the grammar, distinguish it from its neighbours, and explain what I noticed.**

---

# Narrative decision: who is known?

## Recommended approach

Do **not** build the book around a named fictional hero or a heavy story-world.

The protagonist is **the class / the reader as a group of discoverers**. The recurring human presences are intentionally light:

- **The Teacher** — sets up conditions for discovery, asks questions, withholds terminology until it is useful, and does not simply announce answers.
- **The Children / We** — sort, disagree, notice, propose rules, test them, revise them, and eventually build the map.
- **A Parent or Other Adult at Home** — appears occasionally in take-home sorting and discussion activities. The adult is a co-investigator, not an answer machine.
- **The Reader** — is increasingly addressed directly and eventually becomes the classifier and explainer.

No child needs a fixed name in Version 1. If later testing shows that named children improve readability, add a very small recurring group only where dialogue needs speakers. Do not let characterization become the content.

## Why

The unfolding should feel as though **the reader is participating in the discovery of the structure**, rather than watching a fictional character discover it. The sentences, comparisons, disagreements, and emerging map are the drama.

---

# The hidden architecture

The reader experiences the book in this order:

**pile → sorting → comparison → structural question → first dimension → controlled contrast → second dimension → third dimension → names → conjunction → 54-cell reveal → neighbour fluency → difficult cases → transfer to life**

Underneath that experience, the invariant formal structure remains:

**6 Transformation Patterns × 3 Completion Topologies × 3 Persistence Modes = 54 coordinates.**

The three questions may be used sequentially as a **navigation procedure**. The book must repeatedly prevent the mistaken inference that the completed form itself is semantically assembled in three sequential stages.

> **The questions help us find the box. They are not instructions for building what happened.**

---

# Reveal schedule

The book should control what the reader knows.

| Phase | Reader knows | Reader does not yet need to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | There is a strange pile of ordinary sentences and some seem alike | 54, the three dimensions, formal names |
| First sorting | Similarity is not enough; reasons for grouping matter | Complete taxonomy |
| First question | Different sentences can share the same *kind of change* | Transformation Pattern terminology at first |
| Six-family reveal | There are six recurrent kinds of transformation | 3 × 3 structure inside each family |
| Second question | Completions differ in what makes them enough | Persistence Modes |
| Topology reveal | Focal / Distributed / Release | Full 54 map until the distinction is usable |
| Third question | Finished results remain in importantly different ways | Full conjunction doctrine in academic language |
| Persistence reveal | Standing / Bearing / Efficacy | Nothing essential is withheld after this point |
| Board reveal | 6 × 3 × 3 = 54; every box has an address | Mastery of all cells |
| Neighbour work | A cell is clarified by nearby alternatives | — |
| Mystery work | Some cases are difficult or genuinely underdetermined | The expectation that everything must fit |
| Transfer | The grammar applies beyond the original strips | — |

# Part I — The Pile

## Chapter 1 — The Bag of Sentences

**Reader state entering:** knows nothing about Persistence.

**Scene / activity:** The Teacher puts a large bag of sentence strips on a table or floor. Children turn them the right way up. The initial sample is curated and manageable, not all 1,307 utterances.

**Purpose:** establish that the raw material is ordinary intelligible language, not technical examples.

**What happens:**

- Children read strips aloud.
- They notice obvious similarities and differences.
- The Teacher asks them to make any arrangement that seems useful.
- No formal categories are supplied.

**Key question introduced:**

> What made you put those together?

**Do not reveal yet:** the number 54; the 15 terms; the three dimensions.

**Placeholder content:**

- [ ] Select 18–24 highly concrete starter strips from Pixels of Clarity.
- [ ] Ensure the set contains obvious but non-keyword-only similarities.
- [ ] Write opening classroom scene in 800–1,200 words.
- [ ] Add printable-card reference.
- [ ] Add one take-home mini-sort of 6 cards.

**Exit criterion:** reader understands that grouping requires a reason, not merely a feeling of similarity.

---

## Chapter 2 — Piles Are Arguments

**Reader state entering:** can make intuitive groups.

**Purpose:** turn sorting into explicit comparison.

**Core insight:** A pile says, “these belong together because…”

**Activity:** two groups sort the same small set differently. Neither is immediately declared wrong.

**Teacher prompts:**

- What is the same about these?
- What is different?
- Which sentence is hardest to place?
- If this card moved, what reason would justify moving it?
- Are you grouping by topic, or by what happened?

**Pedagogical move:** introduce the difference between **what a sentence is about** and **the structural movement that makes it intelligible** without yet naming completed intelligibility.

**Placeholder content:**

- [ ] Create two plausible competing sorts.
- [ ] Include one deliberately seductive topic-based grouping.
- [ ] Write discussion showing how a structural grouping outperforms a topic grouping.
- [ ] Add “Your turn” exercise.

**Exit criterion:** reader begins asking what *happened structurally* rather than what noun/topic appears.

# Part II — What Changed?

## Chapter 3 — The First Question

**Purpose:** make the first dimension necessary before naming it.

**Question:**

> **What kind of unresolvedness changed?**

Child-facing bridge wording may begin as:

> **What kind of change happened?**

and become more exact as the chapter develops.

**Activity:** use six small clusters selected so that each cluster strongly exhibits one Transformation Pattern while varying topology and persistence enough to prevent superficial template learning.

**Do not give the six names immediately.** Let children propose descriptions first.

**Placeholder content:**

- [ ] Select 3–5 strong utterances for each of the six transformation families.
- [ ] Write six “what keeps happening in this pile?” discussions.
- [ ] Record child-generated provisional labels before formal labels appear.

**Exit criterion:** reader can distinguish at least several different *kinds of transformation* without formal vocabulary.

# Part III — When Is It Enough?

## Chapter 5 — Same Change, Different Finish

**Purpose:** create the need for Completion Topology.

**Method:** hold Transformation Pattern as stable as possible and compare triplets that differ primarily in topology.

Start with the clearest transformation family, probably Repair or Opening, after corpus review.

**Teacher question:**

> These are all the same kind of change. Why do they become complete in different ways?

**Provisional child contrasts:**

- one decisive locus;
- completion across a constitutive plurality;
- something that governed the situation loses its determining force.

**Placeholder content:**

- [ ] Build at least four topology contrast triplets from different transformations.
- [ ] Test them for keyword leakage.
- [ ] Include a case where “one” appears but the form is not Focal.
- [ ] Include a plural sentence that is not Distributed.
- [ ] Include disappearance/removal that is not Release.

**Exit criterion:** reader can feel the three topology differences before formal names are supplied.

## Chapter 6 — Three Ways a Change Becomes Complete

### Focal

### Distributed

**Invariant definition:** The transformation becomes complete only across a constitutive plurality, span, sequence, range, reciprocal field, set of cases, or system. No single locus is sufficient by itself.

**Anti-keyword warning:** Distributed does not merely mean “lots of things.”

### Release

# Part IV — What Have We Got Now?

## Chapter 7 — Three Repairs That Are Not the Same

**Purpose:** make Persistence Mode perceptible through minimal contrast.

**Anchor contrast:**

- “That part is fixed now.”
- “This part is right because it fits the guide.”
- “Fixing that part makes it work again.”

All are Repair; hold topology stable where corpus validation permits. Ask:

> **What is different about what remains once the repair is complete?**

**Placeholder content:**

- [ ] Validate the anchor triplet against exact cell assignments.
- [ ] Build at least four persistence-mode triplets across different transformations/topologies.
- [ ] Write the discussion before introducing Standing/Bearing/Efficacy.

**Exit criterion:** reader distinguishes determination, constitutive bearing, and operative consequence/capacity in ordinary language.

# Part V — The Map Appears

## Chapter 9 — Three Questions, One Thing

**Purpose:** teach Dimensional Conjunction before revealing the complete board as a multiplication table.

**Core teaching:**

A coordinate does not mean:

> Transformation property + Topology property + Persistence property.

The three dimensions simultaneously constrain **one completed form**.

Use one familiar cell and show why removing/changing any coordinate changes the form itself.

**Required line:**

> **The questions help us find the box. They are not instructions for building what happened.**

**Activities:**

- Change one dial and ask what must change in the utterance family.
- Try to create a bad “three-feature bundle” and diagnose why it fails.
- Ask whether a sentence could sit unchanged in a neighbouring cell.

**Placeholder content:**

- [ ] Select one especially clear conjunction demonstration.
- [ ] Create one deliberately Cartesian bad example.
- [ ] Create a three-dial visual metaphor only if it does not imply sequential construction.

**Exit criterion:** child understands the difference between a search route and the unity of the thing being classified.

## Chapter 10 — The Fifty-Four Boxes

**This is the major reveal.**

The class now has:

- six answers to Question 1;
- three answers to Question 2;
- three answers to Question 3.

Now show:

**6 × 3 × 3 = 54.**

Reveal the full board as an explanatory compression of distinctions the reader already knows.

**Activity:** physically/digitally place already-familiar cards into their exact addresses.

**Do not present 54 empty definitions to memorize.** Each position should appear occupied by a small family of familiar utterances.

**Placeholder content:**

- [ ] Design printable 54-box board.
- [ ] Design responsive digital board.
- [ ] Select initial 3–5 anchor utterances per cell.
- [ ] Add links from each cell to its larger Pixels of Clarity family.
- [ ] Decide whether cell numbers are visible by default or secondary metadata.

**Exit criterion:** reader can explain why the board has exactly 54 positions and navigate to a position using all three dimensions.

# Part VI — Learning the Neighbourhoods

## Chapter 11 — Move One Box

**Purpose:** make relational discrimination the central fluency exercise.

A cell is partly clarified by what its neighbours are not.

**Game mechanic:** hold two coordinates fixed, change one.

Examples:

- same Transformation + same Topology; compare Standing/Bearing/Efficacy;
- same Transformation + same Persistence; compare Focal/Distributed/Release;
- same Topology + same Persistence; compare the six Transformation Patterns.

**Teacher question:**

> What is the smallest structural change that makes this card belong next door?

**Placeholder content:**

- [ ] Build 12 high-quality neighbour sets.
- [ ] Include adversarial cases where vocabulary is misleading.
- [ ] Add “explain why not the neighbour” scoring rule.

**Exit criterion:** classification is justified by structural exclusion, not familiarity.

# Part VII — Keep the Problem Alive

## Chapter 13 — The Mystery Boxes

**Purpose:** teach that a grammar is investigated, not merely obeyed.

Use structurally unstable or contested material only after the reader has a stable map.

Initial known review area:

- **Cells 25–27: Relating × Release × Standing/Bearing/Efficacy.**

The corpus has shown disagreement about what is losing governance in this family. Do not silently resolve this for pedagogical neatness.

**Activity:** present competing utterance clusters and ask:

- What does each cluster seem to think was governing the opening situation?
- Which interpretation preserves Relating as the Transformation Pattern?
- Which neighbouring cell would capture the rejected interpretation?
- Is the coordinate underdetermined, is an utterance wrong, or is our formulation of a primitive too loose?

## Chapter 14 — No Box Yet

**Purpose:** prevent forced classification.

Persistence rule:

> **No completion, no coordinate.**

Give fragments, ongoing situations, promises, questions, and events where the operative demand has not yet been discharged.

Ask children to distinguish:

- “I cannot tell which box” from
- “there is no completed form here yet.”

**Placeholder content:**

- [ ] Create 10 clear no-completion examples.
- [ ] Create 5 deceptive examples that sound finished grammatically but are structurally unresolved.

**Exit criterion:** learner does not assume every sentence must occupy a coordinate.

---

## Chapter 15 — Clear Does Not Mean True

**Purpose:** separate intelligibility from truth/correctness.

A completed intelligibility may be false, mistaken, bad, or incorrect.

**Activity:** pair structurally identical completions where one claim is true and one false, or one repair is correct and one mistaken, without making factual knowledge the lesson.

**Core line:**

> The grammar tells us how something became clear enough to judge. It does not tell us that the judgment is right.

**Placeholder content:**

- [ ] Develop age-appropriate examples that avoid contentious factual material.
- [ ] Connect back to “completed enough to judge.”

**Exit criterion:** coordinate assignment is not confused with endorsement.

---

# Part VIII — Structureacy

## Chapter 16 — Find One in the Wild

**Purpose:** transfer from curated corpus to ordinary life.

Children bring examples from:

- conversations;
- stories;
- games;
- school problems;
- decisions;
- repairs;
- explanations;
- arrangements;
- family life.

**Homework:** bring one sentence or situation and attempt a coordinate. A parent/adult may disagree and write down why.

**Class discussion:** classify the *governing completion* rather than every local movement inside a complex sentence.

**Placeholder content:**

- [ ] Teacher guide for handling examples with multiple local completions.
- [ ] Simple governing-completion test.
- [ ] Parent take-home sheet.

**Exit criterion:** learner transfers the system beyond wording seen in the book.

---

## Chapter 17 — Make the Sentence

**Purpose:** move from recognition to generation.

Give a coordinate and ask the child to invent:

- a companion utterance;
- a tiny everyday situation;
- a misleading near-neighbour utterance;
- an explanation of the difference.

This is a strong mastery test because the learner must generate from structure rather than classify by resemblance.

**Placeholder content:**

- [ ] 12 coordinate-generation challenges from easy to difficult.
- [ ] Peer-review rubric based on the three questions.

**Exit criterion:** child can deliberately instantiate a coordinate.

---

## Chapter 18 — The Whole Picture

**Purpose:** consolidate the mental model without turning the ending into a glossary dump.

Return to the original bag of strips.

The same apparent mess can now be seen as an organized structural field.

The reader should be able to say, in substance:

> There are six kinds of unresolvedness that can be transformed. There are three structural ways the transformation can count as complete. There are three ways the achieved completion can remain available. Those constraints meet in 54 positions. I can use three questions to navigate the map, but the thing I find is one unified completion. I can compare neighbouring boxes, say when there is no completion yet, and use the map on things I have never seen before.

Only here, if useful, give the ability its pedagogical name:

# Structureacy

**Final activity:** classify a fresh mixed set, defend several difficult choices, identify one no-coordinate case, and create one original example.

**Final image:** the 54-box map, now populated rather than empty.

# Authoring rules

1. **Experience before terminology.** Whenever possible, make a distinction perceptible before naming it.
2. **Contrast before definition dump.** Teach dimensions through neighbouring examples.
3. **Ordinary language first.** Formal terms name things the reader has already begun to notice.
4. **No keyword classifiers.** Include counterexamples to obvious lexical shortcuts.
5. **Do not force symmetry.** Cell families need not have identical numbers or sentence templates.
6. **Keep the formal definitions as invariants.** Child formulations are interfaces, not replacements.
7. **Preserve conjunction.** Never leave the reader thinking a cell is three detachable features.
8. **Allow no-coordinate judgments.** Do not reward forced placement.
9. **Separate intelligibility from truth.** Classification is not endorsement.
10. **Keep problems alive.** Flag genuine instability rather than silently editing it away.
11. **Make children explain exclusions.** “Why not the neighbouring box?” is often more diagnostic than “why this box?”
12. **Transfer is required.** Familiar-card accuracy alone is not mastery.
13. **The classroom is scaffolding, not subject matter.** The book is about the grammar.
14. **The reader becomes the investigator.** Adult figures should recede as competence grows.

# Editorial production plan

## Pass 1 — Corpus-to-concept

- Derive candidate child formulations of all 15 primitives from the relevant cell families.
- Test each formulation against every cell containing that primitive.
- Record counterexamples and leakage into sibling primitives.

## Pass 2 — Contrast library

- Build minimal neighbour sets.
- Prioritize contrasts where only one coordinate changes.
- Remove sets that depend on vocabulary rather than structure.

## Pass 3 — Teaching deck

- Select the smallest credible set of utterances that can induce the distinctions.
- Keep the full corpus available as reserve and validation material.

## Pass 4 — Chapter drafting

Draft Parts I–IV first. Do not write the 54-cell catalogue until the fifteen child formulations and contrast sets are stable.

## Pass 5 — Board and cell pages

Populate all 54 positions with selected utterance families and neighbour cues.

## Pass 6 — Mystery and transfer material

Add contested cells, no-completion cases, truth/intelligibility distinctions, and real-life transfer.

## Pass 7 — Classroom testing

Observe where children:

- create unexpected piles;
- confuse dimensions;
- rely on keywords;
- cannot explain exclusions;
- disagree productively;
- spontaneously use the map on new material.

Revise pedagogy without silently changing the underlying grammar.
