# Pixels of Clarity — Narrative Arc v3

> **Status:** authoritative narrative spine for the ten-year-old pedagogical edition after Chapter 7.
>
> **Governing rule:** the book is not merely a sequence of explanations. It is a sequence of discoveries that changes what the reader is capable of wondering about.

## The central movement

The book still begins with a mess of ordinary sentences and one simple challenge:

> **Can we find a good reason for putting some of these together?**

But after Chapter 7, the book changes character.

The child has now discovered enough structure to notice that the same distinctions recur across wildly different situations. That recurrence creates a new kind of pressure:

> **Why does this keep showing up?**

From this point onward, the book must preserve both:

- a **linear prerequisite spine**, because some distinctions genuinely depend on earlier ones; and
- a **nonlinear inquiry field**, because new understanding creates questions that should be allowed to bend the route.

The resulting movement is:

**mess → piles → arguments → six kinds of change → three shapes of completion → three ways a result remains → wonder about recurrence → naming the third dimension → one thing seen three ways → the 54-position map → neighbour fluency → hard cases → limits of classification → intelligibility and truth → transfer → generation → structureacy**

---

# Narrative laws

## 1. One new necessity per chapter

Each chapter should still do one main job:

1. begin with a problem the reader can already enter;
2. let the current method become insufficient;
3. compare the failure;
4. discover one new distinction or one new question;
5. name it only when naming helps;
6. end with an unresolved pressure that makes the next chapter necessary.

## 2. The learner-state outranks the old syllabus

The old chapter order is not sacred.

When the child has genuinely acquired a new way of seeing, the book should ask:

> **What becomes worth wondering about now?**

If a question becomes unavoidable because of what the child has already learned, the book may yield to it before resuming the formal sequence.

## 3. Wonder must be earned

Do not manufacture philosophical atmosphere.

A philosophical question belongs only when the preceding chapters have produced evidence that makes the question natural.

The governing test is:

> **Could a thoughtful ten-year-old reasonably ask this because of what they have just discovered?**

## 4. Do not convert wonder into doctrine

The book must not announce claims such as:

> “These structures are fundamental to reality.”

Instead, let the recurrence of the patterns create the suspicion that something deeper may be going on.

Prefer:

> **Why do the same structures keep appearing in such different things?**

Leave some questions open.

## 5. Keep the dimensions clean

When discovering or naming one dimension, do not use another dimension to do its explanatory work.

The child may eventually see all three simultaneously, but the route into them must remain discriminable.

## 6. Agency continues to move away from the teacher

The teacher begins as the source of the useful question.

By Chapter 7, the children are already writing the structural questions themselves.

From here:

**teacher arranges evidence → children formulate questions → reader carries questions independently**

The teacher may admit uncertainty, defer questions, or ask what evidence would help.

## 7. Some questions should remain live

Not every philosophical question requires an answer.

Use four dispositions:

- **answer now** — enough machinery exists;
- **test it** — gather examples before deciding;
- **keep it open** — the question is productive without resolution;
- **not yet** — later concepts are required.

## 8. The 54 boxes are still a reveal, not a dump

The full map should still feel like a compression of distinctions the reader already knows.

The philosophical bend must deepen the need for the map, not replace it.

# Chapters 1–7: established path

## Chapter 1 — The Bag of Sentences

A physical mess becomes a problem of comparison.

Key realization:

> **A pile is a claim about what is the same.**

## Chapter 2 — Piles Are Arguments

Topic sorting and structural sorting are separated.

Key tools:

> **What is the same?**
>
> **What is the smallest difference?**

## Chapter 3 — The First Question

The children discover six recurrent kinds of change before formal naming.

Question:

> **What kind of change happened?**

## Chapter 4 — Six Ways Something Can Change

The six families receive the names:

- Differentiation
- Opening
- Relating
- Repair
- Integration
- Settlement

The success of Question 1 creates the need for Question 2.

## Chapter 5 — Same Change, Different Finish

Transformation is held steady.

Children discover three shapes of sufficiency:

- ENOUGH HERE
- ENOUGH ACROSS IT
- NOT IN CHARGE ANYMORE

## Chapter 6 — Three Ways a Change Becomes Complete

The three shapes receive the names:

- Focal
- Distributed
- Release

Question 2 becomes:

> **What made that change count as complete?**

The success of Questions 1 and 2 creates the need for Question 3.

## Chapter 7 — What Is Left When It Is Done?

Transformation and Completion Topology are held steady.

Children discover three unnamed ways an achieved result can remain:

- the achieved determination itself;
- a result constituted through a bearing among things;
- a result remaining as capacity, effect, function, or changed continuation.

The children themselves write:

> **What kind of change happened?**
>
> **What made that change enough?**
>
> **How does the achieved result remain available?**

And then ask:

> **Are those the three questions?**

That question marks the bend.

# The bend: the reader begins to wonder about the structure itself

At this point, the child has seen the same distinctions recur across repairs, routes, arguments, measurements, teams, rules, arrangements, and decisions.

The natural pressure is no longer only classificatory.

Questions now become possible such as:

- Why does this keep showing up in such different things?
- Are we sorting sentences, or are sentences showing us something about understanding?
- Did we invent these patterns, or did we notice them?
- Are these patterns in language, in situations, in thought, or in all three?
- Have we been noticing these all our lives without names for them?
- Does every intelligible thing have one of these structures?
- Can something make sense and still be false?
- Are the boxes in the world, or are they part of how we organize what happens?

The book should not answer all of these now.

It should acknowledge that they have become legitimate questions.

# Revised chapters 8–18

## Chapter 8 — Why Does This Keep Showing Up?

**This replaces the old immediate naming chapter.**

### Opening situation

The children review examples from the first seven chapters and notice how different the topics are.

A wheel, a route, an argument, a score, a team, a rule, a measurement, and an agreement have all responded to the same structural questions.

Someone asks:

> **Why does this work on all of them?**

### Central question

> **Are we only sorting sentences, or are the sentences showing us something more general about how things become intelligible?**

### What the children do

They test the three questions on examples that are not sentence cards first: a broken toy being fixed, a game rule changing, a disagreement ending, a route opening, a recipe coming together.

Then they compare the event with several possible descriptions of the same event.

### What the teacher does

Very little interpretation.

The teacher asks:

- What evidence would count for one answer or another?
- Are you talking about the words, the event, or the way the event has been made intelligible?
- Which questions can we answer now, and which should we keep?

### Discovery

The children discover that the grammar is not simply a word-sorting trick.

But they do **not** conclude that it is metaphysically fundamental.

### Questions to keep open

> Are these structures in the world or in our understanding?

> Did we invent them or notice them?

### Formal content introduced

No new formal term is required.

The chapter's function is to make philosophical reflection an earned consequence of structural learning.

### Why here

This is the first point at which the child has enough repeated evidence to ask the question honestly.

---

## Chapter 9 — Three Ways the Result Remains

Return to the unfinished distinction from Chapter 7.

The child has had one chapter to notice that the third difference also survives changes of subject matter.

Now name:

- Standing
- Bearing
- Efficacy

Child-facing cores:

**Standing** — the achieved determination itself remains available.

**Bearing** — the achieved result remains what it is through a constitutive relation, dependency, contrast, condition, support, frame, source, or mutual determination.

**Efficacy** — the achieved result remains as capacity, function, operative effect, changed continuation, rule, or consequential difference in what can happen.

Break the shortcuts:

- Standing ≠ merely “it stayed”;
- Bearing ≠ merely “there is a relationship”;
- Efficacy ≠ merely “something useful happened.”

At the end, the children now possess all 15 formal primitive terms.

Someone notices:

> Six answers. Three answers. Three answers.

The number 54 is close, but do not reveal the board yet.

---

## Chapter 10 — Three Questions, One Thing

Now confront the danger created by using three questions sequentially.

Core line:

> **The questions help us find the box. They are not instructions for building what happened.**

The children use one familiar example and change one answer at a time.

They learn that the dimensions are not detachable features stuck onto an event after the fact.

The three dimensions simultaneously constrain one completed intelligibility.

This chapter also returns to the philosophical bend:

> If the questions are different, why do they belong together in one thing?

Do not over-answer.

The formal concept is **Dimensional Conjunction**.

---

## Chapter 11 — The Fifty-Four Boxes

Reveal:

**6 × 3 × 3 = 54**

The board arrives as a compression of discoveries already made.

It should already contain familiar examples.

The reveal should feel like:

> **This is the map we have been building without knowing it.**

Then immediately introduce one inquiry box:

> **Does every intelligible thing have to land somewhere on this board?**

Do not answer yet.

That question will become important later.

---

## Chapter 12 — Move One Dial

Neighbour discrimination becomes the main game.

Hold two coordinates fixed; move one.

Central question:

> **Why here rather than next door?**

This chapter turns the board from a taxonomy into a field of contrast.

The children learn that cells are partly intelligible through their neighbours.

A philosophical pressure also appears:

> If changing one dial changes the whole form, what exactly is the box describing?

Keep the question available without forcing an ontology.

---

## Chapter 13 — Every Box Can Speak

Populate the 54 positions with companion utterance families from Pixels of Clarity.

The discovery is that a cell is not one sentence.

It is a structural attractor capable of many ordinary realizations.

The child begins to feel the grammar as a living space rather than a chart.

This chapter also makes explicit:

> Different words can realize the same structure.

> Similar words can realize different structures.

---

## Chapter 14 — The Hard Boxes

Introduce difficult and unstable regions, including the Relating × Release area.

Teach the difference among:

- weak evidence;
- neighbour confusion;
- genuine ambiguity;
- underdetermination;
- a possible problem in the wording or model.

The teacher now models intellectual honesty:

> **We may not know yet.**

This is where philosophical inquiry becomes disciplined rather than decorative.

---

## Chapter 15 — Sometimes There Is No Box Yet

Return to the question planted at the 54-box reveal:

> **Does everything have to fit?**

Teach:

> **No completion, no coordinate.**

An unfinished event, fragment, open question, or still-live demand need not be forced into a box.

This chapter is also about the limits of a classificatory impulse.

The grammar is a tool for completed intelligibility, not a demand that everything be completed.

---

## Chapter 16 — Clear Does Not Mean True

A philosophical question from the bend now receives the machinery it needed:

> **Can something make sense and still be wrong?**

Yes.

Teach the distinction between intelligibility and truth.

A claim can be structurally determinate and still be false, mistaken, unfair, or bad.

The grammar describes how something becomes determinate enough to be judged; it does not certify the judgment.

This is one of the major payoffs of allowing the earlier question to remain open.

---

## Chapter 17 — Catch It in the Wild / Make One for a Box

Combine transfer and generation rather than treating them as isolated end-stage exercises.

First, children collect examples from ordinary life:

- conversations;
- games;
- repairs;
- decisions;
- explanations;
- rules;
- stories;
- disagreements;
- arrangements.

Then reverse the direction:

Give a coordinate and ask the learner to invent an utterance or situation that belongs there.

Test the invented example against neighbours.

This chapter answers a practical version of the philosophical question:

> **Is the grammar only in our classroom cards?**

The answer is tested through transfer rather than asserted.

---

## Chapter 18 — The Whole Picture

Return to the original bag.

The sentences are ordinary.

The reader is not.

The child should now be able to explain:

- completed intelligibility;
- the three questions;
- the 15 formal terms;
- why there are 54 positions;
- why neighbouring positions differ;
- why the three dimensions are one conjunction rather than three stages;
- why some cases remain difficult;
- why some things have no coordinate yet;
- why intelligibility is not truth;
- why the same structures can be tested across ordinary life.

Only here foreground the broader ability as **structureacy**.

The final philosophical note should remain modest:

> We began by sorting sentences.
>
> We ended by discovering a new way to ask what makes things intelligible.
>
> How far that way of seeing reaches is now a question the reader is equipped to investigate.

Do not close that question for them.

# Recurring inquiry devices from Chapter 8 onward

These should be used sparingly and only when earned.

## Keep This Question

A question that is now legitimate but not yet answerable.

Example:

> **Are the boxes in the world, or in the way we understand the world?**

## Test It Somewhere Else

Take a structural distinction into a new domain and see whether it survives.

## What Would Count as Evidence?

Use this when children begin making broad claims about the grammar.

## Not Yet

Explicitly mark questions that require later machinery.

This teaches that postponing an answer is not the same as avoiding a question.

# The revised 18-chapter shape

The book still fits naturally into 18 chapters, but the second half is no longer a straight march through the taxonomy.

1. The Bag of Sentences
2. Piles Are Arguments
3. The First Question
4. Six Ways Something Can Change
5. Same Change, Different Finish
6. Three Ways a Change Becomes Complete
7. What Is Left When It Is Done?
8. **Why Does This Keep Showing Up?**
9. Three Ways the Result Remains
10. Three Questions, One Thing
11. The Fifty-Four Boxes
12. Move One Dial
13. Every Box Can Speak
14. The Hard Boxes
15. Sometimes There Is No Box Yet
16. Clear Does Not Mean True
17. Catch It in the Wild / Make One for a Box
18. The Whole Picture

The chapter count remains 18 because the philosophical bend replaces and recombines parts of the old route rather than merely adding material on top.

If drafting later shows that one of these chapters carries two genuine necessities, split it. The pedagogical logic outranks the number 18.

# Drafting check from Chapter 8 onward

Before writing a chapter, answer:

1. What exact unresolved pressure is inherited from the previous chapter?
2. What can the reader now see that they could not see three chapters ago?
3. What would a thoughtful ten-year-old naturally wonder at this point?
4. Is that wondering earned by evidence already encountered?
5. Which formal Persistence commitment, if any, becomes necessary because of that question?
6. What should be answered now, tested, kept open, or deferred?
7. Is the teacher doing work the children could now do themselves?
8. Does the chapter preserve structural precision while allowing genuine philosophical space?
9. What new question becomes possible by the end?

If those answers are not clear, do not draft the chapter yet.

---

# The book in one sentence

A class begins with a bag of ordinary sentences and, by learning to sort, compare, discriminate, and question what makes them intelligible, discovers a 54-position grammar—and then begins to wonder what it means that the same structures keep appearing across ordinary life.
