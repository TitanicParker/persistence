# Pixels of Clarity — Narrative Arc

> **Status:** working narrative spine for the ten-year-old pedagogical edition.
>
> **Rule:** the story is the discovery of the structure. The classroom is the setting, not the subject.

## The central dramatic question

The book begins with a mess of ordinary sentences and one apparently simple challenge:

> **Can we find a good reason for putting some of these together?**

Everything that follows should feel like an answer forced by the previous difficulty.

The children should never learn a new part of Persistence because the book has reached the next section of a syllabus. They learn it because the sorting problem has become impossible to solve without a sharper distinction.

The narrative therefore has one continuous movement:

**mess → piles → arguments → recurring changes → three completion shapes → three persistence modes → one 54-position map → neighbour fluency → difficult cases → ordinary life**

---

# Five-act arc

## Act I — The mess becomes a question

### Chapter 1 — The Bag of Sentences

A large pile of ordinary sentence strips is tipped out. The children sort freely. Some arrangements are sensible, but the teacher keeps asking one question:

> **What made you put those together?**

The chapter ends when the children realize that a pile is a claim about what is the same.

**Do not yet teach:** topic versus structure in depth, six transformations, 54 cells, or formal terminology.

**Narrative job:** turn a physical mess into a problem of comparison.

### Chapter 2 — Piles Are Arguments

Two groups sort the same cards differently. Both sorts make sense. This creates the real problem: a grouping can be reasonable and still be wrong for the question we care about.

The children discover the distinction between:

- what a sentence is **about**;
- what **happened** in it.

They also learn two working tools:

> **What is the same?**
>
> **What is the smallest difference?**

The chapter ends because the children now need a stable first question:

> **What kind of change happened?**

**Narrative job:** make the first dimension necessary.

---

## Act II — The first six families appear

### Chapter 3 — The First Question

The class uses only the question **What kind of change happened?** on carefully chosen contrasts. Six recurrent families begin to appear, but they are not yet named.

The children invent provisional labels in their own language.

**Narrative job:** let the six families be seen before they are named.

### Chapter 4 — Six Ways Something Can Change

The six families receive their formal names:

- Differentiation
- Opening
- Relating
- Repair
- Integration
- Settlement

Each name arrives as a compression of something already recognized.

The children can now reduce a new sentence from the whole field to one of six broad structural families.

Then a new problem appears: sentences inside the **same** family still do not all finish in the same way.

**Narrative job:** the success of the first distinction reveals the need for the second.

---

## Act III — Completion gets its shape

### Chapter 5 — Same Change, Different Finish

Hold the transformation steady. Compare close examples.

The children notice three different answers to:

> **What made that change enough?**

They experience the distinction before learning the names.

**Narrative job:** make Completion Topology necessary.

### Chapter 6 — One Place, Across the Field, or No Longer in Charge

Name the three completion topologies:

- Focal
- Distributed
- Release

The child-facing contrasts are initially:

- completion gathers around a decisive locus;
- completion only exists across a constitutive plurality;
- completion occurs because something that governed the situation no longer determines it.

This chapter must firmly break the false shortcuts:

- Focal ≠ merely one thing;
- Distributed ≠ merely many things;
- Release ≠ merely something disappearing.

At the end, a remaining difficulty becomes visible: two sentences can share both Transformation and Topology yet still leave different kinds of result.

**Narrative job:** make Persistence Mode necessary.

---

## Act IV — The map appears

### Chapter 7 — What Is Left When It Is Done?

Use minimal contrasts to reveal the third question:

> **How does the achieved result remain available?**

The three recurring possibilities become perceptible before being named.

**Narrative job:** let Standing, Bearing, and Efficacy be felt.

### Chapter 8 — Three Ways the Result Remains

Name:

- Standing
- Bearing
- Efficacy

The child now has all fifteen primitives and all three kinds of question.

The chapter ends with an obvious numerical curiosity:

> Six answers to the first question. Three to the second. Three to the third. What does that make?

**Narrative job:** make the map reveal feel earned.

### Chapter 9 — Three Questions, One Thing

Before showing the board, establish Dimensional Conjunction.

The three questions are a search route, not three stages from which the event is assembled.

Required line:

> **The questions help us find the box. They are not instructions for building what happened.**

Use one familiar coordinate and change one dial at a time so the children can hear the whole form change.

**Narrative job:** prevent a Cartesian misunderstanding immediately before the map appears.

### Chapter 10 — The Fifty-Four Boxes

Reveal:

**6 × 3 × 3 = 54**

The board is not shown as 54 empty definitions. Familiar sentences already occupy it.

The revelation should feel like:

> **Oh. This is the map we have been building without knowing it.**

**Narrative job:** convert accumulated distinctions into one memorable mental picture.

---

## Act V — From map to fluency

### Chapter 11 — Move One Dial

Neighbour comparisons become the main game. Hold two coordinates fixed and change one.

The question changes from **Where does this go?** to:

> **Why does it go here rather than next door?**

**Narrative job:** replace recognition with discrimination.

### Chapter 12 — Every Box Can Speak

Each cell is introduced through a compact family of ordinary utterances drawn from Pixels of Clarity.

The cell is not one sentence. It is a structural attractor that can speak in several natural ways.

**Narrative job:** make the 54 positions feel inhabited and human rather than taxonomic.

### Chapter 13 — The Hard Boxes

Introduce cells whose utterance families are unstable or contested, including the Relating × Release region.

Do not conceal uncertainty. Teach children to distinguish:

- a bad clue;
- a genuine neighbour problem;
- a structurally ambiguous case;
- a possible problem in the map or its wording.

**Narrative job:** show that structureacy includes disciplined uncertainty.

### Chapter 14 — Sometimes There Is No Box Yet

Teach:

> **No completion, no coordinate.**

A sentence fragment, unfolding event, or unresolved situation need not be forced into the system.

**Narrative job:** prevent classification compulsion.

### Chapter 15 — Clear Does Not Mean True

Teach the distinction between completed intelligibility and truth.

A claim can have a clear structural form and still be false, mistaken, unfair, or bad.

**Narrative job:** prevent the map from becoming an authority stamp.

### Chapter 16 — Catch It in the Wild

Move away from prepared cards. Use conversations, stories, games, repairs, decisions, explanations, rules, disagreements, and ordinary events.

Children collect examples and defend their placements.

**Narrative job:** transfer.

### Chapter 17 — Make a Sentence for a Box

Reverse the task. Give a coordinate and ask the child to invent an utterance or situation that belongs there.

Then test it against neighbours.

**Narrative job:** move from recognition to generation.

### Chapter 18 — The Whole Picture

Return to the original bag. What once looked like a pile now looks like a structured field.

The reader should be able to explain:

- the three questions;
- the 15 terms;
- why there are 54 boxes;
- why neighbouring boxes differ;
- why not everything must be classified;
- why intelligibility is not truth.

Only here name the broader ability explicitly as **structureacy**.

**Narrative job:** make the ending mirror the beginning: same ordinary language, radically changed seeing.

# Character and voice discipline

The book should not become a story about a charismatic teacher.

The teacher starts the process and asks useful questions, but should progressively recede. Across the book, agency should move:

**teacher asks → children ask each other → reader asks independently**

The children need not be named in Version 1. Brief voices such as “someone said” or “another child disagreed” are enough unless later testing shows that named speakers materially improve comprehension.

The real protagonist is the reader's changing ability to see structure.

---

# Repetition discipline

Every chapter should introduce **one new necessity**.

Do not reteach the previous chapter's discovery at length. Begin from it, use it, and create the next problem.

Each chapter should therefore have the same deep rhythm without feeling formulaic:

1. Start with a problem the reader already knows how to enter.
2. Let the current method fail or become insufficient.
3. Compare the failure carefully.
4. Discover the next distinction.
5. Give the distinction a name only when useful.
6. End with a new problem that makes the next chapter necessary.

That is the narrative engine of the book.

---

# Reveal discipline

The reader should not be told future answers simply to motivate them.

Before Chapter 4: do not name the six Transformation Patterns.

Before Chapter 6: do not name Focal, Distributed, Release.

Before Chapter 8: do not name Standing, Bearing, Efficacy.

Before Chapter 10: do not show the complete 54-box map.

Before Chapter 18: use “structureacy” sparingly or not at all. Let the ability precede the label.

The pleasure of the book depends partly on the reader repeatedly realizing:

> **We already found this. Now we have a name for it.**

---

# The book in one sentence

A class begins with a bag of ordinary sentences and, by repeatedly asking what makes some belong together and others not, discovers the fifteen distinctions and fifty-four positions of Persistence until the map becomes a way of seeing ordinary life.
