# Pixels of Clarity — Narrative Arc v2

> **Status:** authoritative narrative spine for the ten-year-old pedagogical edition.
>
> **Governing rule:** the story is the discovery of the structure. The classroom is the setting, not the subject.

## The central dramatic question

The book begins with a mess of ordinary sentences and one apparently simple challenge:

> **Can we find a good reason for putting some of these together?**

Every new idea must be forced by a difficulty created by the previous method.

The book must never feel like a syllabus in disguise.

Its movement is:

**mess → piles → arguments → six kinds of change → three shapes of completion → three ways a result remains → one 54-position map → neighbour fluency → difficult cases → ordinary life**

The ending returns to the same kind of ordinary sentences as the beginning. What changes is the reader.

---

# Narrative laws

## 1. One new necessity per chapter

Each chapter should do one job:

1. begin with a problem the reader can already enter;
2. let the current method become insufficient;
3. compare the failure;
4. discover one new distinction;
5. name it only when naming helps;
6. end with the next unresolved problem.

Do not reteach the previous chapter at length.

## 2. Keep the dimensions clean while they are being discovered

When teaching **Transformation Pattern**, do not explain examples using vocabulary that belongs primarily to Completion Topology or Persistence Mode.

In particular, avoid teaching Repair as “the fault no longer governs” during the discovery phase, because **de-governance is the later signature of Release**.

For children, the first-dimension contrast should stay close to:

- Differentiation — a difference becomes available enough to matter;
- Opening — something becomes possible, reachable, available, or continuable;
- Relating — how things stand with or depend on each other becomes part of the answer;
- Repair — something failing relative to how it should be is put right enough;
- Integration — separate parts acquire roles in an organized whole;
- Settlement — something still calling for an answer or continuation is brought to sufficiency.

The adult invariant definitions may be shown later or in side notes, but the discovery narrative must not make one dimension secretly do the work of another.

Likewise, while discovering Topology, do not smuggle in Standing/Bearing/Efficacy as the explanation. While discovering Persistence Mode, hold the first two coordinates as steady as possible.

## 3. Examples must earn their place

Every teaching example should have a declared job:

- **anchor** — makes a family easy to see;
- **contrast** — distinguishes a neighbour;
- **trap** — exposes keyword or topic matching;
- **test** — checks transfer on fresh wording;
- **mystery** — preserves genuine ambiguity.

Avoid examples that accidentally require two new distinctions at once.

## 4. Agency must move away from the teacher

The teacher begins as the person who asks the useful question.

Across the book, agency should move:

**teacher asks → children ask one another → reader asks independently**

By Chapter 3, children should already be challenging one another's piles.

By Chapter 5, the teacher should mostly arrange contrasts and ask for reasons.

By Chapter 10, the class should be able to navigate the board largely without teacher interpretation.

By the final act, the teacher can almost disappear.

## 5. Do not reveal the map before the reader has built the distinctions

Before Chapter 4: do not give the six formal Transformation names.

Before Chapter 6: do not name Focal, Distributed, Release.

Before Chapter 8: do not name Standing, Bearing, Efficacy.

Before Chapter 10: do not show the complete 54-box map.

Before the final chapter: use **structureacy** sparingly or not at all.

The recurring pleasure should be:

> **We already found this. Now we have a name for it.**

# Five-act arc

## Act I — The mess becomes a question

### Chapter 1 — The Bag of Sentences

A pile of ordinary sentence strips is tipped out. Children sort freely.

The only recurring question is:

> **What made you put those together?**

The chapter ends when they discover:

> **A pile is a claim about what is the same.**

**Do not yet teach:** topic-versus-structure explicitly, formal dimensions, six families, or the number 54.

**Narrative job:** turn a physical mess into a problem of comparison.

### Chapter 2 — Piles Are Arguments

Two groups sort the same cards differently. Both sorts make sense.

This forces the distinction between:

- what a sentence is **about**;
- what **happened** in it.

Children acquire two tools:

> **What is the same?**
>
> **What is the smallest difference?**

The chapter ends because they need a stable structural question:

> **What kind of change happened?**

**Narrative job:** make the first dimension necessary.

---

## Act II — Six kinds of change

### Chapter 3 — The First Question

Use only:

> **What kind of change happened?**

and, when needed:

> **What was unresolved before, and what is different now?**

Six recurrent families emerge from carefully chosen, topology-neutral and persistence-neutral examples.

Children invent provisional descriptions. The teacher does not supply a formal definition for each family yet.

The crucial movement is from **teacher-led recognition** to **children comparing candidate piles against one another**.

**Do not introduce yet:** formal names, “governing” vocabulary, cell addresses, topology, persistence.

**Narrative job:** let the six families be seen before they are named.

### Chapter 4 — Six Ways Something Can Change

Now name:

- Differentiation
- Opening
- Relating
- Repair
- Integration
- Settlement

Each formal name must be attached to a family the children already recognize.

The chapter should spend more time on **neighbour contrasts** than on exposition.

Preferred child-facing formulations:

- **Differentiation:** a difference becomes clear or usable enough to matter.
- **Opening:** something becomes available, reachable, possible, or able to continue.
- **Relating:** how things stand with or depend on one another becomes part of the answer.
- **Repair:** something failing relative to how it should be is put right enough.
- **Integration:** separate parts gain roles in an organized whole.
- **Settlement:** something that still called for an answer, decision, explanation, or continuation is brought to sufficiency.

The chapter ends with examples that are unmistakably the **same Transformation Pattern** but nevertheless complete differently.

**Narrative job:** success with Question 1 exposes the need for Question 2.

---

## Act III — Completion gets its shape

### Chapter 5 — Same Change, Different Finish

Hold Transformation Pattern stable.

Start with the clearest family, probably Repair after corpus validation.

Compare examples where the transformation is constant but the reason it counts as complete changes.

Ask:

> **What made that change enough?**

Do not give the three formal names yet.

Children should discover three recurring shapes:

- completion gathers around a privileged locus;
- completion requires a constitutive plurality or field;
- completion occurs because a prior determining constraint loses its force.

**Narrative job:** make Completion Topology necessary.

### Chapter 6 — Three Ways a Change Becomes Complete

Name:

- Focal
- Distributed
- Release

Break the keyword shortcuts explicitly:

- Focal ≠ merely one thing;
- Distributed ≠ merely many things;
- Release ≠ merely removal or disappearance.

The key child-level idea for Release is:

> **Something that used to be in charge of how the situation went is no longer in charge.**

At the end, hold both Transformation and Topology steady and expose a remaining difference in what persists.

**Narrative job:** make Question 3 necessary.

---

## Act IV — The result remains, then the map appears

### Chapter 7 — What Is Left When It Is Done?

Use close triplets where Transformation and Topology are held as steady as possible.

Ask:

> **How does the achieved result remain available?**

Children discover three recurring possibilities before naming them.

**Narrative job:** let Standing, Bearing, and Efficacy be felt.

### Chapter 8 — Three Ways the Result Remains

Name:

- Standing
- Bearing
- Efficacy

Child-facing cores:

- **Standing:** the achieved determination itself is what remains.
- **Bearing:** the result remains what it is through how things stand with, depend on, contrast with, or support one another.
- **Efficacy:** the result remains as something that now works, acts, enables, changes, or governs what can happen next.

Break the shortcuts:

- Standing ≠ merely “it stayed”;
- Bearing ≠ merely “there is a relationship”;
- Efficacy ≠ merely “something useful happened.”

End with the numerical curiosity:

> Six answers. Three answers. Three answers. What does that make?

**Narrative job:** make the map reveal inevitable.

### Chapter 9 — Three Questions, One Thing

Before the full map is shown, establish Dimensional Conjunction.

Required line:

> **The questions help us find the box. They are not instructions for building what happened.**

Use one familiar cell and change one coordinate at a time. The child should hear that the whole completed form changes.

**Narrative job:** prevent a Cartesian misunderstanding before the map appears.

### Chapter 10 — The Fifty-Four Boxes

Reveal:

**6 × 3 × 3 = 54**

The board should already contain familiar examples.

It must feel like:

> **This is the map we have been building without knowing it.**

Do not present 54 empty definitions.

**Narrative job:** compress the discoveries into one memorable mental picture.

---

## Act V — From map to fluency

### Chapter 11 — Move One Dial

Neighbour comparisons become the main game.

Hold two coordinates fixed. Change one.

The central question becomes:

> **Why here rather than next door?**

**Narrative job:** move from recognition to discrimination.

### Chapter 12 — Every Box Can Speak

Each cell is introduced through a compact family of ordinary utterances from Pixels of Clarity.

A cell is not one sentence. It is a family of natural crystallizations of one structural form.

**Narrative job:** make the 54 positions inhabited rather than taxonomic.

### Chapter 13 — The Hard Boxes

Introduce unstable or contested regions, including Relating × Release.

Teach the difference among:

- poor evidence;
- neighbour confusion;
- genuine ambiguity;
- a possible problem in the wording or model.

**Narrative job:** teach disciplined uncertainty.

### Chapter 14 — Sometimes There Is No Box Yet

Teach:

> **No completion, no coordinate.**

Do not force fragments or unresolved events into the grammar.

**Narrative job:** prevent classification compulsion.

### Chapter 15 — Clear Does Not Mean True

Teach that intelligibility is not truth.

A structurally complete claim can still be false, mistaken, unfair, or bad.

**Narrative job:** prevent the grammar becoming an authority stamp.

### Chapter 16 — Catch It in the Wild

Move beyond prepared cards into ordinary life: conversation, games, stories, decisions, repairs, rules, explanations, disagreements.

Children collect and defend examples.

**Narrative job:** transfer.

### Chapter 17 — Make a Sentence for a Box

Reverse the task.

Give a coordinate. Ask the learner to generate an utterance or situation, then test it against neighbours.

**Narrative job:** move from recognition to generation.

### Chapter 18 — The Whole Picture

Return to the original bag.

The sentences are ordinary. The reader's seeing is not.

The reader should now be able to explain:

- the three questions;
- the 15 terms;
- why there are 54 positions;
- why neighbouring positions differ;
- why some cases remain difficult;
- why not everything must be classified;
- why intelligibility is not truth.

Only here foreground the broader ability as **structureacy**.

**Narrative job:** make the ending mirror the beginning.

# Drafting check before every new chapter

Before writing a chapter, answer:

1. What exact problem is inherited from the previous chapter?
2. What does the reader already know that can be used without re-explaining it?
3. What one new distinction becomes necessary?
4. Are the examples dimension-clean for the distinction being taught?
5. Is the teacher solving anything the children could discover by comparison?
6. What is the smallest set of examples that makes the distinction visible?
7. What unresolved problem will pull the reader into the next chapter?

If those seven answers are not clear, do not draft the chapter yet.

---

# The book in one sentence

A class begins with a bag of ordinary sentences and, by repeatedly asking what makes some belong together and others not, discovers the fifteen distinctions and fifty-four positions of Persistence until the map becomes a way of seeing ordinary life.
