# Constraint Grammar of Completed Intelligibility

## Exhaustive Glossary and Terminological Reference

This glossary fixes the preferred vocabulary of the mature theory. It is organized by theoretical level rather than by historical order. Entries distinguish primitive architecture from derived structure, historical formation, atlas representation, methodology, and formal notation. Where older vocabulary remains useful only as a historical or informal gloss, that status is stated explicitly.

The governing hierarchy is: **coordinate-generated form → canonical crystallization → concrete manifestation**. The governing compositional rule is: **Transformation Pattern + Completion Topology + Persistence Mode → coordinate-generated phenomenological syntax**. Capitalization matters where needed, especially **Opening** (Transformation Pattern) versus **opening** (historical phase).

## How to use this glossary

- Use **Preferred status** to identify the theoretical level of a term.
- Treat cross-references as part of the definition: neighboring terms are deliberately kept distinct.
- Prefer mature terms in new theoretical writing; use legacy terms only for historical discussion or explicitly marked informal glosses.
- Do not infer metaphysical exhaustiveness from the finite atlas. The current theory establishes an internally coherent 54-position generative architecture, not a proof that no other completed forms are possible.
- When the unqualified word **form** would be ambiguous, specify **completed form**, **coordinate-generated form**, **canonical crystallization**, or **concrete manifestation**.

## I. Core architecture

### Constraint Grammar of Completed Intelligibility
**Preferred status:** Cross-cutting

**Definition.** The mature theoretical framework that models recurrent ways in which a structured incompletion becomes sufficiently complete for determinate uptake. Its central generative rule composes a Transformation Pattern, a Completion Topology, and a Persistence Mode into a coordinate-generated phenomenological syntax.

**Function in the theory.** Names the theory as a whole and identifies its primary explanatory commitment: completed forms are generated by the simultaneous satisfaction of three recurring constraints rather than collected as unrelated semantic categories.

**Distinguish from.** Not ordinary linguistic grammar, not a taxonomy of subject matters, and not a proof that the 54-position space is metaphysically exhaustive.

**Relations.** See completed intelligibility; coordinate grammar; constraint grammar; state-space; phenomenological syntax; coordinate-generated form.

### completed intelligibility
**Preferred status:** Cross-cutting

**Definition.** The condition in which a situation has become sufficiently determinate, relative to its governing opening pressure, to support a stable next uptake without owing the same unresolved work that governed its opening.

**Function in the theory.** Names the phenomenon the theory seeks to model. It is the target condition whose recurring forms are organized by the three primitive dimensions.

**Distinguish from.** Not truth, correctness, certainty, finality, or permanent closure. A false, revisable, fictional, provisional, or later-reopened result may nevertheless be complete in the relevant sense.

**Relations.** See opening pressure; completion; sufficiency; determinate uptake; completed form; intelligibility event.

### completed form
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The structural organization of a completed intelligibility event considered at the level of what changed, how the change became sufficient, and how the achieved result remains available.

**Function in the theory.** Provides the general object type instantiated by coordinate-generated forms, canonical crystallizations, and concrete manifestations at different levels of specificity.

**Distinguish from.** Do not use as a synonym for canonical crystallization. A completed form may be described more abstractly than any canonical name.

**Relations.** See coordinate-generated form; canonical crystallization; concrete manifestation; form.

### intelligibility event
**Preferred status:** Phenomenological

**Definition.** A transition in which an unresolved situation undergoes a structurally relevant change, reaches sufficient completion, and leaves an achieved result available for further uptake.

**Function in the theory.** Supplies the event-like ontology implicit in the generated sentences: there is a before, a decisive change, a condition of sufficiency, and a post-completion mode of availability.

**Distinguish from.** Not restricted to sentences or conscious deliberation; sentences are a privileged observational case, not the ontology of the theory.

**Relations.** See historical formation; opening pressure; governing transformation; landing; persistent result.

### unresolved situation
**Preferred status:** Phenomenological

**Definition.** A situation that is already structured enough to place a determinate demand on further uptake but has not yet satisfied that demand.

**Function in the theory.** Provides the opening field against which transformation and completion can be identified.

**Distinguish from.** Not sheer absence, chaos, or ignorance. Unresolvedness in this theory is patterned and therefore constraining.

**Relations.** See opening pressure; historical opening; completion; intelligibility event.

### opening pressure
**Preferred status:** Phenomenological

**Definition.** The structured incompletion that determines what kind of change would answer the situation. It is the negative contour of the completion to come: missing difference, restricted availability, absent bearing, governing misfit, unorganized plurality, or residual demand.

**Function in the theory.** Links the unresolved field to the Transformation Pattern by specifying what is still owed.

**Distinguish from.** Distinguish from Opening, the Transformation Pattern that enlarges availability; and from historical opening, the initial phase of an event.

**Relations.** See unresolved situation; Transformation Pattern; Differentiation; Opening; Relating; Repair; Integration; Settlement.

### determinate uptake
**Preferred status:** Phenomenological

**Definition.** A subsequent act of understanding, judgment, response, use, continuation, or incorporation that can proceed because the situation has become sufficiently complete in the relevant respect.

**Function in the theory.** States the practical criterion for completed intelligibility without requiring truth or absolute closure.

**Distinguish from.** Not identical with agreement, belief, action, or correctness; uptake may be critical, revising, or rejecting.

**Relations.** See completed intelligibility; sufficiency; persistent result.

### completion
**Preferred status:** Cross-cutting

**Definition.** The achievement of sufficiency relative to the opening pressure that governed the event. Completion occurs when the relevant transformation has acquired the topology required to count as enough and leaves a persistent result.

**Function in the theory.** Connects transformation, topology, and persistence into one completed intelligibility event.

**Distinguish from.** Not mere stopping, temporal ending, or exhaustion. A process may stop without completing, and a completion may remain dynamically active.

**Relations.** See sufficiency; Completion Topology; landing; Settlement.

### sufficiency
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The condition under which a transformation counts as enough to answer its governing opening pressure. In the grammar, sufficiency is specified primarily by Completion Topology.

**Function in the theory.** Explains why the same Transformation Pattern can yield different completed forms: different events may require focal concentration, distributed constitution, or release from prior governance before the change counts as complete.

**Distinguish from.** Not quantitative adequacy and not truth. It is structural enoughness relative to the event's unresolved demand.

**Relations.** See Completion Topology; Focal; Distributed; Release; completion.

### structural determination
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The degree to which the three coordinate constraints restrict the phenomenological syntax of a completed form before any particular semantic crystallization or wording is chosen.

**Function in the theory.** Explains why coordinate triples can generate recognizable local forms prospectively and why some triples are more constraining than others.

**Distinguish from.** Not semantic individuation, lexical naming, or empirical uniqueness.

**Relations.** See semantic individuation; semantic freedom; constraint strength; admissible region; coordinate-generated form.

### semantic individuation
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The further specification by which a coordinate-generated region takes on a particular recognizable semantic form, typically expressed by a canonical crystallization.

**Function in the theory.** Accounts for the difference between what a coordinate requires structurally and which specific local phenomenon is selected as its canonical realization.

**Distinguish from.** Not structural determination and not linguistic realization. Semantic individuation chooses a phenomenon within an admissible region; linguistic realization chooses how to phrase it.

**Relations.** See canonical crystallization; semantic freedom; linguistic realization; admissible region.

### semantic freedom
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The remaining latitude of semantic realization after the three coordinate constraints have been jointly satisfied.

**Function in the theory.** Provides the positive interpretation of variation in constraint strength. It permits one generated syntax to support more than one semantically specific crystallization without weakening the coordinate architecture.

**Distinguish from.** Not arbitrariness. The admissible realizations remain bounded by the coordinate-generated form.

**Relations.** See Open realization; Tightly constrained; width of an admissible region; semantic individuation.

### phenomenological syntax
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The event-shaped structural pattern generated by a coordinate: a transformation-pattern, a completion-pattern, and a persistence-pattern organized into one intelligible movement.

**Function in the theory.** Names what is generated before canonical naming. It is 'syntax' because recurrent functional roles combine systematically, and 'phenomenological' because the roles concern how completion is experienced and organized rather than linguistic word order.

**Distinguish from.** Not ordinary grammatical syntax and not the canonical cell name.

**Relations.** See coordinate-generated sentence; generated syntax G(c); constraint grammar; conjugation.

### coordinate-generated form
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The most abstract local completed form demanded by a specific Transformation Pattern × Completion Topology × Persistence Mode triple, stated without importing a narrower canonical semantic choice.

**Function in the theory.** Serves as the primary local theoretical object. It makes the coordinate's structural demands explicit and allows comparison with canonical crystallization and concrete manifestation.

**Distinguish from.** Not a canonical crystallization and not a concrete example. It is more specific than the coordinate labels alone but more abstract than a selected semantic realization.

**Relations.** See coordinate-generated sentence; canonical crystallization; structural determination; admissible region.

### canonical crystallization
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** A selected, stable, semantically specific realization of a coordinate-generated form, given a canonical name so the region becomes memorable and operationally usable.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a reference realization for the atlas without being mistaken for the entire coordinate-required region.

**Distinguish from.** Not the coordinate-generated form itself, especially for Open realization coordinates. It is also not identical with a concrete manifestation, which is an instance in particular material.

**Relations.** See canonical name; crystallization; coordinate-generated form; concrete manifestation; constraint strength.

### concrete manifestation
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** A particular case in which a coordinate-generated form, usually through a canonical crystallization, is realized in specific semantic material.

**Function in the theory.** Shows cross-domain recurrence and provides experiential evidence that one structural form can appear in different contents.

**Distinguish from.** Not a definition of the form and not the canonical crystallization itself.

**Relations.** See manifestation; strong example; semantic individuation; linguistic realization.

### linguistic realization
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** The wording by which a coordinate-generated form, crystallization, or manifestation is expressed in natural language.

**Function in the theory.** Separates theoretical structure from phrasing, allowing paraphrase, translation, and stylistic variation without automatically implying a change of coordinate.

**Distinguish from.** Not semantic individuation. Two wordings may realize the same crystallization, while two crystallizations may share similar wording.

**Relations.** See companion utterance; coordinate-generated sentence; translation audit; semantic individuation.

### admissible region
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** The set-like region of phenomenological possibilities consistent with the simultaneous constraints of a coordinate triple. In formal shorthand it is denoted A(c).

**Function in the theory.** Models the fact that a coordinate may determine a narrow or wider family of realizations rather than a single uniquely forced semantic object.

**Distinguish from.** Not yet a mathematically specified set with an independently defined metric or measure. It is a disciplined formal abstraction.

**Relations.** See A(c); admissible family; width of an admissible region; constraint strength; intersection.

### constraint strength
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The degree to which a coordinate triple narrows its admissible region before canonical crystallization.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a principled way to distinguish near-forced local forms from coordinates that remain structurally clear but semantically broader.

**Distinguish from.** Not confidence in classification, empirical frequency, evidential weight, or importance of the form.

**Relations.** See Near-determinate; Tightly constrained; Open realization; structural determination; width of an admissible region.

### Near-determinate
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The strongest constraint-strength class: the coordinate triple leaves little freedom beyond wording, domain, or minor local mechanism, so generated form and canonical crystallization nearly coincide.

**Function in the theory.** Marks coordinates whose semantic region is very narrow under the current atlas.

**Distinguish from.** Not logically unique or metaphysically necessary. 'Near' is essential: the theory does not claim a proof of unique realization.

**Relations.** See constraint strength; Tightly constrained; Open realization.

### Tightly constrained
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The intermediate constraint-strength class: the coordinate triple defines a narrow admissible family, while the canonical crystallization contributes a meaningful but strongly guided semantic sharpening.

**Function in the theory.** Allows the atlas to preserve a vivid canonical form without treating its full specificity as directly forced by the coordinates.

**Distinguish from.** Not the same as Near-determinate, and not an Open realization in which the remaining semantic choice is substantially wider.

**Relations.** See constraint strength; canonical crystallization; semantic individuation.

### Open realization
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The broadest constraint-strength class: the coordinate fixes a recognizable phenomenological syntax but leaves several materially distinct semantic realizations plausible before canonical selection.

**Function in the theory.** Makes semantic latitude an explicit property of the state-space rather than a defect or classification failure.

**Distinguish from.** Not 'weakly constrained' in the sense of arbitrary. Transformation, topology, and persistence remain jointly binding.

**Relations.** See semantic freedom; admissible region; canonical crystallization; constraint strength.

### form
**Preferred status:** Cross-cutting

**Definition.** A context-sensitive shorthand whose meaning must be qualified. Preferred specific uses are completed form, coordinate-generated form, canonical crystallization, or concrete manifestation.

**Function in the theory.** Prevents a common ambiguity in which different theoretical levels are collapsed under the single word 'form'.

**Distinguish from.** Do not use unqualified 'form' where the distinction among generated structure, canonical realization, and manifestation matters.

**Relations.** See completed form; coordinate-generated form; canonical crystallization; concrete manifestation.

### intelligibility before truth
**Preferred status:** Phenomenological

**Definition.** The theoretical priority according to which a situation must become determinate enough to be judged before truth, correctness, justification, or success can be assessed.

**Function in the theory.** Keeps the grammar focused on conditions of intelligible completion rather than epistemic endorsement.

**Distinguish from.** Not a denial of truth or correctness and not a claim that intelligibility guarantees either.

**Relations.** See completed intelligibility; determinate uptake.

### local-to-global resolution
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** The problem of explaining how multiple local operations and local completions yield one governing completed coordinate at the selected global scale.

**Function in the theory.** Motivates governance diagnostics and the distinction between formation history and completed form.

**Distinguish from.** Not a separate primitive dimension.

**Relations.** See governance; local completion; global completion; governing transformation.

### translation
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The production of a new linguistic realization in another language or register, potentially preserving the same coordinate-generated form despite changes in wording and grammar.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a domain for testing structural invariance across realization systems.

**Distinguish from.** Not assumed to preserve every semantic nuance or landing automatically.

**Relations.** See translation audit; linguistic realization; invariance.

### structural preservation
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The retention of relevant coordinate-level organization across paraphrase, translation, editing, or transformation of a manifestation.

**Function in the theory.** Supplies the criterion by which changed wording can still count as realization of the same generated form.

**Distinguish from.** Not lexical identity or total semantic equivalence.

**Relations.** See invariance; translation audit; structural editing.

### coordinate invariance
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The preservation of the same Transformation Pattern, Completion Topology, and Persistence Mode across different manifestations or linguistic realizations.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a strong form of structural preservation and supports comparative analysis.

**Distinguish from.** Not a claim that every detail of formation history is identical.

**Relations.** See invariance; path tolerance; translation audit.

### operative effect
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** A completed consequence that changes what can happen, continue, stop, govern, function, or be accomplished in the field.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a plain-language gloss for Efficacy in specific manifestations.

**Distinguish from.** Not necessarily ongoing activity.

**Relations.** See Efficacy; persistent result.

## II. Primitive dimensions

### Transformation Pattern
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** The primitive dimension that specifies what kind of unresolvedness is decisively altered between the opening and completed situation.

**Function in the theory.** Constrains the transformation verb of the generated syntax. Its six values are Differentiation, Opening, Relating, Repair, Integration, and Settlement.

**Distinguish from.** Not Completion Topology, which specifies what makes the change sufficient, and not Persistence Mode, which specifies how the achieved result remains available.

**Relations.** See opening pressure; governing transformation; T.

### Completion Topology
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** The primitive dimension that specifies the structural shape by which a Transformation Pattern becomes sufficient as completion.

**Function in the theory.** Constrains the topology of enoughness: completion may gather around a privileged locus, require a constitutive plurality, or occur because a prior governor loses its hold.

**Distinguish from.** Not ordinary spatial orientation or direction. 'Topology' here is structural and phenomenological rather than a claim of mathematical topology in the technical sense.

**Relations.** See Focal; Distributed; Release; sufficiency; K.

### Persistence Mode
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** The primitive dimension that specifies in what mode an achieved completion remains available for subsequent uptake.

**Function in the theory.** Constrains the grammar of the residue: the result may remain as Standing, through Bearing, or as Efficacy.

**Distinguish from.** Not grammatical aspect, ontological substance, or a simple static-to-dynamic scale.

**Relations.** See Standing; Bearing; Efficacy; persistent result; P.

## III. Transformation Patterns

### Differentiation
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** A Transformation Pattern in which a difference that was unavailable, flattened, conflated, unmarked, or non-operative becomes determinate enough to matter.

**Function in the theory.** Answers an opening pressure of structural indifference by making a criterion operative.

**Distinguish from.** Not mere objective difference, comparison, or noticing. The difference must alter how the field can be taken up.

**Relations.** See Transformation Pattern; Distinction (legacy); Focal distinguished standing; Field separation.

### Opening
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** A Transformation Pattern in which availability, reach, continuation, access, or possibility becomes greater than under the opening constraint.

**Function in the theory.** Answers restriction, blockage, singular continuation, confinement, interruption, or boundedness by making more available.

**Distinguish from.** Do not confuse with historical opening or opening pressure. Capitalized Opening is a transformation family, not simply the beginning of an event.

**Relations.** See Transformation Pattern; Expansion (legacy); historical opening; Source field; Released option.

### Relating
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** A Transformation Pattern in which a bearing, dependency, ordering, correspondence, reciprocity, response, or mutual determination becomes constitutive of the situation.

**Function in the theory.** Answers insufficient bearing by making how terms relate part of what the completed field is.

**Distinguish from.** Not the generic fact that things can be related, and not Bearing as a Persistence Mode. Relating is the decisive change; Bearing is one way a result can persist.

**Relations.** See Transformation Pattern; Relation (legacy); Bearing; Direct bearing; Reciprocal shift.

### Repair
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** A Transformation Pattern in which an organization failing relative to an operative criterion is redirected, reweighted, replaced, reversed, or released from the fault.

**Function in the theory.** Answers governed misfit by changing the organization so the failure no longer controls the result.

**Distinguish from.** Not generic improvement, refinement, or moral correction. A criterion and a structurally relevant misfit are required.

**Relations.** See Transformation Pattern; Correction (legacy); Focal adjustment; Disorder released.

### Integration
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** A Transformation Pattern in which parts acquire determinate roles through organization into a whole and thereby gain structure, mutual support, or joint capacity.

**Function in the theory.** Answers unorganized plurality by changing what the elements are through their participation in a larger organization.

**Distinguish from.** Not aggregation or mere co-presence. Integration is role-forming.

**Relations.** See Transformation Pattern; Focal whole; Role array; Joint capacity.

### Settlement
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** A Transformation Pattern in which a live demand for further uptake, choice, explanation, adjudication, completion, or continuation is discharged.

**Function in the theory.** Answers residual demand by making the situation no longer owe the same next step.

**Distinguish from.** Not inactivity, accidental stopping, or permanent finality. Settlement is indexed to a governing demand.

**Relations.** See Transformation Pattern; Rest (legacy); Path completion; Continuation discharge.

## IV. Completion Topologies

### Focal
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** A Completion Topology in which sufficiency gathers around a privileged locus such as a selected item, source, target, connection, whole, pivot, endpoint, or settlement point.

**Function in the theory.** Specifies asymmetric concentration: one locus has a privileged relation to why the transformation counts as complete.

**Distinguish from.** Not necessarily spatial centrality, smallness, or singular content. A complex whole can be focal if one locus organizes the completion.

**Relations.** See Completion Topology; Toward (legacy); focal completion; topological rhythm.

### Distributed
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** A Completion Topology in which a constitutive plurality, span, sequence, range, reciprocal field, set of cases, or system belongs to what makes the transformation sufficient.

**Function in the theory.** Requires completion to hold through or across the relevant multiplicity; no single locus is enough by itself.

**Distinguish from.** Not merely 'many things happen'. The plurality must be constitutive of completion.

**Relations.** See Completion Topology; Across (legacy); topological rhythm; distributed completion.

### Release
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** A Completion Topology in which completion occurs because something that governed the opening field no longer governs it.

**Function in the theory.** Models de-governance: conflation, restriction, mismatch, fault, imbalance, compulsion, or residual demand loses determining force, enabling a new organization to count as complete.

**Distinguish from.** Not spatial movement away, escape in a literal sense, or simple removal. The defining fact is loss of governance.

**Relations.** See Completion Topology; Away (legacy); governance; de-governance; topological rhythm.

### de-governance
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The loss of determining force by a condition, constraint, fault, conflation, imbalance, or demand that previously organized the field.

**Function in the theory.** Provides the positive conceptual core of Release and prevents Release from being misunderstood as mere spatial direction.

**Distinguish from.** Not the absence of all structure; a new completed organization usually becomes possible precisely because the old governor no longer rules.

**Relations.** See Release; governance; Settlement; Repair.

## V. Persistence Modes

### Standing
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** A Persistence Mode in which the achieved determination itself remains available as the primary residue of completion: 'there it is'.

**Function in the theory.** Specifies completed availability as achieved presence or standing determination.

**Distinguish from.** Not physical stasis. A standing route, relation, option, or whole may remain dynamic or later change.

**Relations.** See Persistence Mode; Is (legacy); persistent result; persistence conjugation.

### Bearing
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** A Persistence Mode in which the achieved result remains what it is only through a constitutive relation, dependency, contrast, source, condition, proportion, frame, support, correspondence, or mutual determination: 'it holds through this'.

**Function in the theory.** Specifies completed availability as relationally sustained or defined.

**Distinguish from.** Not Relating as a Transformation Pattern and not any ordinary relation. Bearing is the mode in which the completion persists.

**Relations.** See Persistence Mode; With (legacy); Relating; persistence conjugation.

### Efficacy
**Preferred status:** Primitive

**Definition.** A Persistence Mode in which the achieved completion remains as an accomplished capacity, rule, effect, changed continuation, function, or consequential difference in what can happen: 'now it works / takes effect'.

**Function in the theory.** Specifies completed availability as achieved consequence rather than mere standing or constitutive relation.

**Distinguish from.** Not continuous activity, motion, or success in an evaluative sense. A boundary can be efficacious while physically still; a completed journey can be efficacious by discharging continuation.

**Relations.** See Persistence Mode; Does (legacy); persistence conjugation; operative effect.

### persistent result
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** The post-landing result that remains available after the decisive completion, analyzed structurally by Persistence Mode.

**Function in the theory.** Connects historical formation to the coordinate grammar by showing what the event leaves behind for subsequent uptake.

**Distinguish from.** Not identical with Persistence Mode itself: persistent result is an event-specific historical outcome; Persistence Mode is the primitive classification of how such outcomes remain available.

**Relations.** See landing; Persistence Mode; Standing; Bearing; Efficacy.

## VI. Composition, state-space, and periodic structure

### coordinate
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** A position in the 6 × 3 × 3 state-space defined by one Transformation Pattern, one Completion Topology, and one Persistence Mode.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a compact representation of the simultaneous constraints governing a completed form.

**Distinguish from.** Not the canonical name, not the coordinate-generated sentence, and not a claim that the position has a unique concrete manifestation.

**Relations.** See coordinate triple; C; state-space; cell.

### coordinate triple
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** An ordered triple c = <t,k,p> with t in T, k in K, and p in P.

**Function in the theory.** Makes explicit that the three coordinates play distinct functional roles and that order matters conceptually even if displayed in a table.

**Distinguish from.** Not an unordered set of labels.

**Relations.** See coordinate; simultaneous constraint satisfaction; Cartesian product.

### state-space
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** The finite product space of all 54 coordinate triples generated by T × K × P.

**Function in the theory.** Represents the full internally constructed space of coordinate positions, supports neighborhood comparisons, and makes periodic organization visible.

**Distinguish from.** Not yet demonstrated to be the exhaustive metaphysical space of all possible intelligibility.

**Relations.** See 6 × 3 × 3 architecture; C; Cartesian product; periodic lattice.

### 6 × 3 × 3 architecture
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** The product organization consisting of six Transformation Patterns, three Completion Topologies, and three Persistence Modes, yielding 54 coordinate positions.

**Function in the theory.** States the finite combinatorial architecture of the mature atlas.

**Distinguish from.** Not itself an argument for exhaustiveness. It reports the structure of the constructed grammar.

**Relations.** See state-space; coordinate; T; K; P; C.

### periodicity
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The recurrence of the same dimensional contribution at regular intervals in the ordered atlas and, more importantly, in the coordinate-generated phenomenological syntax.

**Function in the theory.** Shows that the 54 forms behave as recombinations of recurring constraints rather than as unrelated names.

**Distinguish from.** Not merely repeated terminology or numbering.

**Relations.** See nested periodicity; transformation rhythm; topological rhythm; persistence rhythm; conjugation.

### nested periodicity
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The superposition of three recurrence frequencies: Transformation Pattern changes every nine positions, Completion Topology every three, and Persistence Mode every position.

**Function in the theory.** Explains the atlas's layered periodic structure and why the generated sentences exhibit recurring deep roles at different scales.

**Distinguish from.** Not a temporal claim about real events recurring at fixed intervals.

**Relations.** See periodicity; periodic lattice; transformation rhythm; topological rhythm; persistence rhythm.

### periodic lattice
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** The ordered representation of the 54-coordinate state-space in which nested recurrence patterns are visible across rows and families.

**Function in the theory.** Makes coordinate substitution and invariance perceptible and supports comparative reading of the generated forms.

**Distinguish from.** The lattice is a representation of the grammar, not its generative cause.

**Relations.** See lattice; state-space; periodicity; constraint grammar.

### lattice
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** A display or relational organization of coordinate positions that makes systematic adjacency, recurrence, and substitution visible.

**Function in the theory.** Supports comparison among neighboring coordinates and reveals periodic structure.

**Distinguish from.** Not used here in the strict order-theoretic sense unless a formal partial order is separately defined. It should not be mistaken for the generative principle itself.

**Relations.** See periodic lattice; state-space; neighboring coordinate.

### grammar
**Preferred status:** Cross-cutting

**Definition.** A system of recurring compositional constraints capable of generating or anticipating structured forms through recombination.

**Function in the theory.** Justifies treating the atlas as more than a catalogue: the same functional slots recur, substitutions preserve invariants, and coordinate triples support prospective derivation.

**Distinguish from.** Not ordinary linguistic grammar unless explicitly discussing language. In this theory, grammar is a structural analogy and explanatory model of completed intelligibility.

**Relations.** See constraint grammar; phenomenological syntax; conjugation.

### constraint grammar
**Preferred status:** Cross-cutting

**Definition.** A grammar in which local forms arise through simultaneous satisfaction of independent but interacting constraints rather than through a linear rewrite sequence.

**Function in the theory.** Names the mature generative principle of the theory.

**Distinguish from.** Not a deterministic algorithm that always maps a coordinate to one unique semantic object.

**Relations.** See simultaneous constraint satisfaction; intersection; admissible region; coordinate-generated form.

### conjugation
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The systematic modulation of one structural combination by substitutions along another recurring dimension, especially the Standing → Bearing → Efficacy sequence within a fixed Transformation Pattern and Completion Topology.

**Function in the theory.** Makes the periodic grammar intuitively legible as a table of recurrently varied completion forms.

**Distinguish from.** An analogy to morphological conjugation, not a claim that the forms are literally linguistic inflections.

**Relations.** See persistence conjugation; topological rhythm; transformation rhythm.

### persistence conjugation
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The three-position sequence obtained by holding Transformation Pattern and Completion Topology fixed while varying Persistence Mode through Standing, Bearing, and Efficacy.

**Function in the theory.** Reveals a recurrent grammar of completed availability: achieved determination, constitutive bearing, achieved consequence.

**Distinguish from.** Not a developmental sequence that every event must traverse.

**Relations.** See conjugation; persistence rhythm; Standing; Bearing; Efficacy.

### transformation rhythm
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The slow nine-position periodic recurrence by which one Transformation Pattern remains fixed while Completion Topology and Persistence Mode vary through their full combinations.

**Function in the theory.** Shows that the central transformation verb is conserved across each nine-cell family.

**Distinguish from.** Not a temporal lifecycle of an individual event.

**Relations.** See nested periodicity; Transformation Pattern; topological rhythm; persistence rhythm.

### topological rhythm
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The three-cell grouping within a Transformation family in which Completion Topology takes Focal, Distributed, and Release, each internally conjugated by the three Persistence Modes.

**Function in the theory.** Shows how the same transformation becomes sufficient by concentration, constitutive plurality, or de-governance.

**Distinguish from.** Not ordinary spatial motion toward/across/away.

**Relations.** See nested periodicity; Completion Topology; Focal; Distributed; Release.

### persistence rhythm
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The fastest recurrence in the atlas: Standing, Bearing, and Efficacy vary every cell within a fixed Transformation Pattern and Completion Topology.

**Function in the theory.** Makes the residue grammar visible across the entire state-space.

**Distinguish from.** Not a temporal sequence of persistence states.

**Relations.** See persistence conjugation; nested periodicity.

### local form
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** A coordinate-specific completed form considered at one position of the state-space, usually at the coordinate-generated or canonical level.

**Function in the theory.** Lets the theory discuss one position without implying that the local form is isolated from periodic relations to other positions.

**Distinguish from.** Not local completion in historical formation, which concerns scale or temporal embedding.

**Relations.** See cell; coordinate-generated form; local completion.

### neighboring coordinate
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** A coordinate that differs from another by one value along one dimension, or by a minimally specified substitution under the chosen representation.

**Function in the theory.** Supports contrastive analysis of what changes when one constraint changes while others are held fixed.

**Distinguish from.** Neighborhood is representational unless a formal distance metric is defined.

**Relations.** See coordinate substitution; lattice; structural movement.

### coordinate substitution
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The controlled replacement of one coordinate value while holding the others fixed in order to reveal the invariant contribution of each dimension.

**Function in the theory.** Provides one of the clearest internal tests of dimensional distinctness and periodic structure.

**Distinguish from.** Not empirical intervention unless implemented in a concrete study.

**Relations.** See neighboring coordinate; invariance; conjugation.

### admissible family
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The family of semantically specific realizations compatible with one coordinate-generated form.

**Function in the theory.** Names the local range within which canonical crystallizations and other possible realizations may fall.

**Distinguish from.** Not synonymous with the full state-space or with all manifestations of one canonical crystallization.

**Relations.** See admissible region; semantic freedom; canonical crystallization.

### width of an admissible region
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** A qualitative description of how much semantic latitude remains after a coordinate's three constraints have been jointly applied.

**Function in the theory.** Underlies the constraint-strength scale from Near-determinate to Open realization.

**Distinguish from.** No numerical metric of width has yet been established; current use is comparative and theoretical.

**Relations.** See constraint strength; admissible region; semantic freedom.

### compositional constraint
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** A condition contributed by one dimension that restricts the form generated by the joint coordinate without by itself determining the whole form.

**Function in the theory.** Clarifies that each axis contributes a distinct requirement whose effect becomes visible in composition with the others.

**Distinguish from.** Not a simple additive feature or independent semantic label.

**Relations.** See simultaneous constraint satisfaction; constraint grammar; intersection.

### simultaneous constraint satisfaction
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** The mode of composition in which a local form must satisfy the Transformation Pattern, Completion Topology, and Persistence Mode at once as one coherent event.

**Function in the theory.** Provides the preferred model of coordinate composition. The three dimensions constrain one another's admissible realization without collapsing into a single axis.

**Distinguish from.** Not a serial procedure in which one dimension is applied after another in time.

**Relations.** See intersection; A(c); compositional constraint; coordinate triple.

### intersection
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** The formal metaphor by which the admissible region of a coordinate is represented as the common region satisfying all three dimensional constraints: A(c) = A_T(t) ∩ A_K(k) ∩ A_P(p).

**Function in the theory.** Expresses the logic of simultaneous restriction and explains why each dimension excludes otherwise plausible realizations.

**Distinguish from.** Not yet a fully specified set-theoretic construction with independently operationalized component sets.

**Relations.** See A(c); simultaneous constraint satisfaction; formal skeleton.

### invariance
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The persistence of a recognizable structural contribution when other coordinate values, semantic domains, or linguistic realizations change.

**Function in the theory.** Supports claims that the dimensions recur independently and that forms can be compared across manifestations.

**Distinguish from.** Not strict mathematical invariance unless a transformation group or formal equivalence relation is defined.

**Relations.** See coordinate substitution; stacking; translation; periodicity.

### structural movement
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** A change from one coordinate position to another produced by altering one or more of the three primitive constraints.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a disciplined way to describe how revisions, paraphrases, or constructions can shift completed form within the state-space.

**Distinguish from.** Not physical movement and not necessarily historical movement inside one event.

**Relations.** See neighboring coordinate; coordinate substitution; state-space.

## VII. Historical formation and governance

### historical formation
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** The temporally or procedurally unfolding path by which an unresolved situation develops into a completed form.

**Function in the theory.** Separates the history that produces a completion from the completed coordinate form that retrospectively compresses that history.

**Distinguish from.** Not the coordinate grammar itself. Many different formation paths may converge on the same coordinate.

**Relations.** See historical grammar; opening; governing transformation; landing; path tolerance.

### historical grammar
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** The recurrent organization of formation through opening, local operations, governing transformation, landing, and persistent result.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a process-level complement to the coordinate grammar.

**Distinguish from.** Not identical with the six Transformation Patterns as a mandatory temporal lifecycle. The mature theory does not require every event to traverse all six transformations in order.

**Relations.** See coordinate grammar; historical formation; local operation; landing.

### coordinate grammar
**Preferred status:** Cross-cutting

**Definition.** The completed-form grammar generated by the three primitive dimensions and represented in the 54-position state-space.

**Function in the theory.** Distinguishes the structural description of a completed event from the historical path by which that event was formed.

**Distinguish from.** Not the same as historical grammar or ordinary linguistic grammar.

**Relations.** See constraint grammar; historical grammar; coordinate-generated form.

### opening
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** The initial historical configuration of an intelligibility event before the governing completion has been achieved.

**Function in the theory.** Provides the concrete starting condition from which opening pressure and subsequent transformations are analyzed.

**Distinguish from.** Lowercase historical opening is distinct from capitalized Opening, the Transformation Pattern of enlarged availability.

**Relations.** See unresolved situation; opening pressure; Opening; historical formation.

### governing transformation
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** The transformation that most strongly determines what the completed event becomes at the global level.

**Function in the theory.** Links historical formation to the Transformation Pattern coordinate by identifying which change controls the event's completed profile.

**Distinguish from.** Not every local operation is governing; local distinctions, relations, repairs, or integrations may support a different global transformation.

**Relations.** See governing movement; governance; local operation; Transformation Pattern.

### governing movement
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** A broader historical description of the highest-governing internal movement by which the event is reorganized toward completion.

**Function in the theory.** Used methodologically to recover the global form from local histories when the governing transformation is not immediately obvious.

**Distinguish from.** Prefer governing transformation when the transformation family itself is being identified. Governing movement may include the surrounding trajectory, not only the transformation label.

**Relations.** See governing transformation; governance; blind historical assay.

### landing
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** The earliest point or span in historical formation at which the governing transformation has become sufficiently determined that the completed topology and persistence mode can be stably recovered.

**Function in the theory.** Acts as the hinge between unfolding formation and completed coordinate form.

**Distinguish from.** Not necessarily one token, instant, or final boundary. Linguistic wrapping or additional material may follow without changing the completed coordinate.

**Relations.** See historical formation; completion; persistent result; wrapping; retrospective stabilization.

### local completion
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** A completion achieved within a constituent subevent, phrase, subsystem, stage, or local scale that may later be incorporated, overridden, or reinterpreted by a larger completion.

**Function in the theory.** Allows nested and recursive formation without treating every local closure as the global coordinate of the whole.

**Distinguish from.** Not global completion and not local form in the state-space.

**Relations.** See local operation; global completion; retrospective stabilization.

### global completion
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** The completion that governs the intelligibility event at the scale selected for analysis and determines its overall coordinate profile.

**Function in the theory.** Provides the target of coordinate recovery when multiple local completions occur within one event.

**Distinguish from.** 'Global' is scale-relative, not metaphysically absolute. A globally completed sentence may be local within a larger discourse.

**Relations.** See local completion; governance; governing transformation.

### local operation
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** A structurally relevant change occurring within a subpart or intermediate stage of historical formation.

**Function in the theory.** Explains how complex histories can contain multiple distinctions, openings, relations, repairs, integrations, or settlements without equating each with the final governing Transformation Pattern.

**Distinguish from.** Not necessarily a coordinate-bearing completion in its own right.

**Relations.** See local completion; governing transformation; historical grammar.

### retrospective stabilization
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** The post-landing reorganization by which earlier local material acquires a more stable role or interpretation in light of the completed whole.

**Function in the theory.** Explains why completion can determine the significance of earlier elements without implying that the earlier experience was unreal.

**Distinguish from.** Not arbitrary hindsight or semantic rewriting; the stabilization must be supported by the completed organization.

**Relations.** See landing; wrapping; path tolerance; retrospective settlement.

### wrapping
**Preferred status:** Historical

**Definition.** Material that occurs after the governing landing but packages, attributes, qualifies, reports, or embeds an already determined completion without necessarily changing its coordinate.

**Function in the theory.** Prevents analysts from treating every final token or clause as structurally governing merely because it is last.

**Distinguish from.** Wrapping can change the coordinate if it actually reorganizes the completion; the term applies only when the prior landing remains governing.

**Relations.** See landing; governance; path tolerance.

### path tolerance
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The capacity of distinct historical formation paths to converge on the same completed coordinate-generated form.

**Function in the theory.** Separates the invariants of completion from transient constructional details of how the result was reached.

**Distinguish from.** Not unlimited indifference to history. Some path changes alter governance, topology, or persistence and therefore change the coordinate.

**Relations.** See compressed invariant; historical formation; coordinate grammar.

### compressed invariant
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The compact structural information conserved from a complex formation history in the completed coordinate: transformation type, completion topology, and persistence mode.

**Function in the theory.** Explains why the coordinate can summarize a rich event without reproducing its entire history.

**Distinguish from.** Not a lossless encoding of all semantic or linguistic information.

**Relations.** See path tolerance; coordinate; structural compression.

### governance
**Preferred status:** Cross-cutting

**Definition.** The relation by which one movement, condition, criterion, or demand has priority in determining the organization and completion of the event at the chosen scale.

**Function in the theory.** Supports selection of governing transformation, analysis of Release as de-governance, and distinction between local and global completion.

**Distinguish from.** Not authority in a social or political sense unless the manifestation itself concerns such authority.

**Relations.** See governing transformation; Release; scope; configurational control; uptake control; counterfactual centrality.

### scope
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The extent of material or scale over which a candidate movement exerts organizing control.

**Function in the theory.** Serves as one criterion for identifying governance: a movement affecting the whole event is a stronger governing candidate than one confined to a local fragment, all else equal.

**Distinguish from.** Not sufficient by itself to establish governance.

**Relations.** See governance; configurational control; uptake control; counterfactual centrality.

### configurational control
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The degree to which a candidate movement determines how other parts are organized, interpreted, or assigned roles within the completed whole.

**Function in the theory.** Provides one diagnostic of governance in historical analysis.

**Distinguish from.** Not mere local salience or surface prominence.

**Relations.** See governance; scope; uptake control; counterfactual centrality.

### uptake control
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The degree to which a candidate movement determines how the completed event can be taken up, continued, interpreted, or acted upon.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a functional diagnostic of which movement governs completion.

**Distinguish from.** Not equivalent to frequency, emphasis, or syntactic headship.

**Relations.** See governance; determinate uptake; counterfactual centrality.

### counterfactual centrality
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The degree to which removing, reversing, or substantially altering a candidate movement would change the completed organization or coordinate of the event.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a counterfactual test for governance and structural participation.

**Distinguish from.** Not proof of causation in the empirical sense; it is a structural diagnostic within the analysis.

**Relations.** See governance; ablation; rival testing; configurational control.

### atlas
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** The organized reference representation of the 54 coordinate positions together with generated forms, canonical crystallizations, utterances, examples, derivations, sentences, and constraint-strength judgments.

**Function in the theory.** Makes the state-space inspectable and supports comparison, decoding, exposition, and generation.

**Relations.** See cell; periodic lattice; coordinate-generated form; canonical crystallization.

### cell
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** A numbered atlas position corresponding to one coordinate triple and its associated generated and canonical reference material.

**Function in the theory.** Provides stable indexing from 1 to 54.

**Distinguish from.** Not an ontological atom or a claim that every manifestation belongs uniquely to one cell.

**Relations.** See coordinate; local form; atlas.

### canonical name
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** The stable lexical label assigned to a canonical crystallization.

**Function in the theory.** Allows concise reference to the selected semantic realization of a coordinate-generated form.

**Distinguish from.** Not the deepest theoretical object and not necessarily uniquely forced by the coordinates.

**Relations.** See canonical crystallization; companion utterance.

### companion utterance
**Preferred status:** Mnemonic

**Definition.** A short ordinary-language utterance that compresses the felt closure of a canonical crystallization.

**Function in the theory.** Provides phenomenological recall and a compact experiential handle on a completed form.

**Relations.** See felt compression; phenomenological compression; canonical crystallization.

### strong example
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** One of the curated manifestations selected to make a coordinate's invariant event-shape vivid across semantically diverse materials.

**Function in the theory.** Supports recognition of structural recurrence and guards against overfitting a form to one semantic domain.

**Distinguish from.** Not empirical validation by itself and not interchangeable with any illustrative example invented ad hoc.

**Relations.** See concrete manifestation; manifestation; plain-language exposition.

### coordinate-forced sentence
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** The atlas sentence that states, as directly as possible, the event-shape jointly demanded by a coordinate triple while minimizing extra canonical semantic commitment.

**Function in the theory.** Operationalizes the coordinate-generated form in sentence form and exposes periodic syntax independently of canonical names.

**Distinguish from.** 'Forced' is graded, not absolute; Open realization coordinates retain meaningful semantic latitude.

**Relations.** See coordinate-generated sentence; coordinate-generated form; constraint strength.

### coordinate-generated sentence
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** A domain-light sentence expressing the phenomenological syntax generated by a coordinate triple. In the mature atlas, the coordinate-forced sentence is the canonical reference instance of this type.

**Function in the theory.** Makes the generative content of a coordinate explicit and supports periodic comparison across all 54 forms.

**Distinguish from.** Not the companion utterance and not the canonical name.

**Relations.** See generated syntax G(c); phenomenological syntax; coordinate-forced sentence.

### plain-language exposition
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** A non-technical unfolding of one coordinate's unresolved field, decisive change, completion sufficiency, persistent result, examples, generated form, canonical crystallization, and felt compression.

**Function in the theory.** Makes the constraint conjunction experientially intelligible without requiring formal notation.

**Distinguish from.** Not a separate theoretical layer and not evidence beyond the atlas data it explains.

**Relations.** See strong example; companion utterance; canonical crystallization.

### manifestation
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** Short form of concrete manifestation: a specific semantic event that realizes a generated or crystallized form.

**Function in the theory.** Supports cross-domain comparison and application.

**Distinguish from.** Not the generated form itself.

**Relations.** See concrete manifestation; strong example.

### crystallization
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** The process or result of giving a coordinate-generated admissible region a stable, semantically specific canonical realization.

**Function in the theory.** Names the transition from structural determination to semantic individuation.

**Distinguish from.** Not the same as manifestation, which instantiates a crystallization in concrete material.

**Relations.** See canonical crystallization; semantic individuation.

### felt compression
**Preferred status:** Mnemonic

**Definition.** The experiential condensation of a completed form into a short phrase or utterance whose force becomes intelligible after the fuller structure is understood.

**Function in the theory.** Explains the role of companion utterances as mnemonic closures rather than definitions.

**Distinguish from.** Not structural compression, which concerns theoretical information preserved in the coordinate.

**Relations.** See companion utterance; phenomenological compression; structural compression.

### phenomenological compression
**Preferred status:** Mnemonic

**Definition.** Compression of a completed event into a concise expression that preserves its characteristic lived or recognizable feel rather than its full structural analysis.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a bridge between technical architecture and ordinary recognition.

**Distinguish from.** Not formal encoding and not necessarily sufficient for classification.

**Relations.** See felt compression; companion utterance.

### structural compression
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** Compression of a rich event-history into the coordinate-level invariants needed to characterize its completed form.

**Function in the theory.** Explains the coordinate as a compact but lossy representation of transformation, topology, and persistence.

**Distinguish from.** Not phenomenological compression and not a claim that all semantic detail is preserved.

**Relations.** See compressed invariant; coordinate; path tolerance.

## IX. Methodological vocabulary

### blind historical assay
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** A method that analyzes the unfolding structure of a frozen sentence or other event before exposing the atlas labels or intended coordinate.

**Function in the theory.** Reduces label-driven confirmation by separating historical measurement from coordinate naming.

**Distinguish from.** Not the theory itself and not required for every use of the atlas.

**Relations.** See delayed naming; historical analysis H(s); constraint decoder.

### constraint decoder
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** A procedure or operator that maps a historical analysis and the source event to one or more candidate coordinate triples, together with uncertainty or rival information when needed.

**Function in the theory.** Operationalizes recovery of the completed coordinate from observed formation.

**Distinguish from.** Not guaranteed to be deterministic or error-free; the mature theory permits partial or unresolved recovery.

**Relations.** See decoder D; blind historical assay; strongest rival; partial recovery.

### delayed naming
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The practice of postponing canonical labels and coordinate names until structural evidence has been described independently.

**Function in the theory.** Reduces circularity and label priming in analysis.

**Distinguish from.** Not equivalent to total blindness to all theory; it is a sequencing discipline.

**Relations.** See blind historical assay; constraint decoder.

### rival testing
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The explicit comparison of a proposed coordinate with the strongest plausible neighboring alternative, using wording, structure, governance, or counterfactual differences that should distinguish them.

**Function in the theory.** Sharpens boundaries and exposes where classification depends on a genuinely decisive feature.

**Distinguish from.** Not required to be repeated in every theoretical exposition; it is a research and adjudication tool.

**Relations.** See strongest rival; bounded rivalry; neighboring coordinate; counterfactual centrality.

### strongest rival
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The alternative coordinate or crystallization that best fits the available evidence apart from the proposed primary interpretation.

**Function in the theory.** Focuses comparison on a genuinely competitive alternative rather than an arbitrary contrast.

**Distinguish from.** Not necessarily the numerically adjacent cell unless that cell is structurally the strongest alternative.

**Relations.** See rival testing; bounded rivalry.

### bounded rivalry
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The policy that rivalry should be considered within a structurally plausible neighborhood rather than across every atlas position indiscriminately.

**Function in the theory.** Keeps adjudication informative and computationally or cognitively tractable.

**Distinguish from.** Not a claim that distant alternatives are impossible; out-of-atlas or broader alternatives may still need disclosure when evidence warrants.

**Relations.** See strongest rival; neighboring coordinate; out-of-atlas disclosure.

### partial recovery
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** An analytical outcome in which one or two coordinate dimensions are recoverable but the full triple remains unresolved.

**Function in the theory.** Preserves uncertainty instead of forcing a cell assignment when evidence is insufficient.

**Distinguish from.** Not failure in the pejorative sense; it is a legitimate output state of the method.

**Relations.** See constraint decoder; out-of-atlas disclosure; ambiguity.

### out-of-atlas disclosure
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** An explicit statement that the analyzed material may not be adequately represented by any available coordinate or crystallization under the current atlas.

**Function in the theory.** Prevents forced closure and preserves the theory's non-exhaustiveness at the methodological level.

**Distinguish from.** Not evidence that the atlas is useless; it marks a boundary case or a possible need for theoretical revision.

**Relations.** See epistemic boundary; partial recovery; exhaustiveness.

### vertical stacking
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The comparison of multiple manifestations or analyses that share one or more coordinate values in order to reveal recurring invariants across semantic domains.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a discovery and validation heuristic for dimensional recurrence.

**Distinguish from.** Not the same as the atlas's horizontal numerical ordering and not itself proof of universality.

**Relations.** See stack; invariance; strong example.

### stack
**Preferred status:** Representational

**Definition.** A set of cases intentionally grouped because they are hypothesized or established to share a structural coordinate feature.

**Function in the theory.** Makes repeated structural contributions easier to compare while varying semantic material.

**Distinguish from.** Not a coordinate family unless the grouping criterion is explicitly coordinate-based.

**Relations.** See vertical stacking; invariance.

### return-path validation
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** A bidirectional test in which material generated or selected from an intended coordinate is independently analyzed and decoded to see whether it returns to the intended structural region.

**Function in the theory.** Tests productive adequacy of the grammar and separation between generation and decoding.

**Distinguish from.** Not a proof of uniqueness; multiple acceptable returns may exist for Open realization coordinates.

**Relations.** See inverse generation; constrained synthesis; decoder D; generator.

### inverse generation
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The construction of candidate manifestations from a specified coordinate rather than recovery of a coordinate from an existing manifestation.

**Function in the theory.** Tests whether the coordinate grammar is genuinely productive rather than merely classificatory.

**Distinguish from.** Not a mathematical inverse function in general, because one coordinate can support many realizations and one manifestation may admit more than one analysis.

**Relations.** See constrained synthesis; generator; return-path validation.

### constrained synthesis
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The generation of a candidate event or sentence by satisfying a target coordinate's transformation, topology, and persistence requirements while allowing free choices in domain and linguistic realization.

**Function in the theory.** Operationalizes the generative direction of the grammar.

**Distinguish from.** Not unrestricted creative writing and not guaranteed to yield a unique canonical crystallization.

**Relations.** See inverse generation; coordinate-generated form; admissible region.

### structural epitome
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** A deliberately economical realization in which each retained element participates materially in producing the intended structural form and little non-participating material remains.

**Function in the theory.** Supports clean demonstrations, generation tests, and ablation studies.

**Distinguish from.** Not synonymous with shortest sentence or stylistic minimalism.

**Relations.** See participation analysis; ablation; constitutive material; conductive material.

### ablation
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** A controlled removal, substitution, displacement, splitting, or scope change used to test whether particular material is structurally necessary for a coordinate or crystallization.

**Function in the theory.** Provides counterfactual evidence about participation, governance, and boundary conditions.

**Distinguish from.** Not merely editing for brevity.

**Relations.** See counterfactual centrality; participation analysis; structural editing.

### participation analysis
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** The assessment of how each component of a manifestation contributes to the governing transformation, completion topology, persistence mode, or historical path.

**Function in the theory.** Separates structurally necessary material from material that merely carries or decorates the form.

**Distinguish from.** Not a fixed syntactic parsing scheme.

**Relations.** See constitutive material; conductive material; decorative material; structural epitome.

### constitutive material
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** Material whose removal or substantial alteration changes the governing structural form, coordinate, or essential crystallization.

**Function in the theory.** Identifies components that directly constitute the intended constraint conjunction.

**Distinguish from.** Not merely salient or semantically important material.

**Relations.** See participation analysis; ablation; counterfactual centrality.

### conductive material
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** Material that carries, connects, or enables the structural movement without itself being the principal locus of the generated form.

**Function in the theory.** Accounts for necessary scaffolding in a manifestation that supports the governing structure.

**Distinguish from.** Not decorative material, because removing it may disrupt the path even if it does not define the coordinate directly.

**Relations.** See participation analysis; constitutive material; decorative material.

### decorative material
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** Material whose removal leaves the governing coordinate and essential structural movement intact, even if style, vividness, or local meaning changes.

**Function in the theory.** Helps distinguish structural necessity from expressive enrichment.

**Distinguish from.** 'Decorative' does not mean worthless; such material may be rhetorically or semantically valuable without being coordinate-constitutive.

**Relations.** See participation analysis; ablation; structural epitome.

### translation audit
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** A comparison of source and translated realizations to determine whether the same transformation, topology, persistence mode, and governing landing are preserved despite lexical and grammatical differences.

**Function in the theory.** Separates structural preservation from literal wording preservation.

**Distinguish from.** Not proof of semantic equivalence in every respect.

**Relations.** See linguistic realization; invariance; structural editing.

### structural editing
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** Editing guided by the intended coordinate and historical movement, with revisions evaluated for whether they preserve, sharpen, or deliberately alter transformation, topology, persistence, and landing.

**Function in the theory.** Provides a practical application of the grammar to revision and design.

**Distinguish from.** Not ordinary copyediting, though both may be combined.

**Relations.** See ablation; coordinate substitution; constrained synthesis.

### ambiguity
**Preferred status:** Methodological

**Definition.** A condition in which available evidence supports more than one structurally plausible coordinate or crystallization without a decisive basis for selection.

**Function in the theory.** Authorizes partial recovery or multiple candidates rather than forced classification.

**Distinguish from.** Not the same as semantic freedom within one coordinate; ambiguity concerns which coordinate or crystallization applies, while semantic freedom concerns latitude inside an already fixed coordinate.

**Relations.** See partial recovery; semantic freedom; strongest rival.

### bidirectionality
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The capacity to move both from manifestation to coordinate through analysis/decoding and from coordinate to candidate manifestation through constrained generation.

**Function in the theory.** Marks a stronger form of theoretical productivity than one-way classification.

**Distinguish from.** Not exact invertibility.

**Relations.** See constraint decoder; inverse generation; return-path validation.

### epistemic boundary
**Preferred status:** Cross-cutting

**Definition.** The explicit limit on what the current construction warrants: internal generative coherence and a 54-position architecture are demonstrated within the project, while metaphysical exhaustiveness, empirical universality, and unique recoverability remain open.

**Function in the theory.** Prevents representational completeness of the atlas from being confused with universal completeness of the phenomenon.

**Distinguish from.** Not a retreat from the positive theory; it marks the current scope of warrant.

**Relations.** See exhaustiveness; universality; out-of-atlas disclosure; primitive.

### exhaustiveness
**Preferred status:** Cross-cutting

**Definition.** The stronger claim that the current coordinate space contains all possible completed forms of intelligibility.

**Function in the theory.** Functions primarily as an open research question and boundary condition on interpretation.

**Distinguish from.** The mature theory does not claim that exhaustiveness has been established.

**Relations.** See epistemic boundary; state-space; universality.

### universality
**Preferred status:** Cross-cutting

**Definition.** The stronger empirical or phenomenological claim that the same primitive dimensions and coordinate space apply across all relevant domains, languages, agents, or forms of intelligibility.

**Function in the theory.** Defines a possible future scope claim for validation.

**Distinguish from.** Not presently established by the internal architecture alone.

**Relations.** See epistemic boundary; cross-domain recurrence; translation audit.

### cross-domain recurrence
**Preferred status:** Derived

**Definition.** The reappearance of the same generated structural form across semantically different materials.

**Function in the theory.** Motivates the use of strong examples and supports the claim that the grammar concerns forms of intelligibility rather than topics.

**Distinguish from.** Not itself proof of universality or exhaustiveness.

**Relations.** See strong example; manifestation; invariance.

## X. Formal vocabulary

### T
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** The finite set of six Transformation Patterns: {Differentiation, Opening, Relating, Repair, Integration, Settlement}.

**Function in the theory.** Provides the transformation coordinate domain in the formal skeleton.

**Distinguish from.** Not a temporal variable.

**Relations.** See Transformation Pattern; C; Cartesian product.

### K
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** The finite set of three Completion Topologies: {Focal, Distributed, Release}.

**Function in the theory.** Provides the topology coordinate domain in the formal skeleton. K is retained as the symbol to avoid collision with C for the full coordinate space.

**Distinguish from.** Not technical mathematical topology.

**Relations.** See Completion Topology; C; Cartesian product.

### P
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** The finite set of three Persistence Modes: {Standing, Bearing, Efficacy}.

**Function in the theory.** Provides the persistence coordinate domain in the formal skeleton.

**Distinguish from.** Not probability and not grammatical person.

**Relations.** See Persistence Mode; C; Cartesian product.

### C
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** The coordinate state-space C = T × K × P containing 54 ordered triples.

**Function in the theory.** Provides the formal domain of coordinate positions.

**Distinguish from.** Not completion itself and not canonical crystallization.

**Relations.** See coordinate; Cartesian product; state-space.

### A(c)
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** The admissible region associated with coordinate c: the family of phenomenological realizations satisfying all three constraints of c.

**Function in the theory.** Represents graded semantic latitude and supports the distinction between structural generation and canonical crystallization.

**Distinguish from.** Not yet a metrically or measure-theoretically specified set.

**Relations.** See admissible region; intersection; width of an admissible region.

### generated syntax G(c)
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** A formal notation for the coordinate-generated phenomenological syntax associated with coordinate c.

**Function in the theory.** Names the domain-light event-description generated from the triple before canonical crystallization.

**Distinguish from.** To avoid overloading G with a generator operator, the preferred prose notation is G(c) only when context is unambiguous; otherwise write generated syntax or use GenSyntax(c).

**Relations.** See coordinate-generated sentence; phenomenological syntax; generator Gen.

### canonical realization r
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** A semantically specific realization r selected from the admissible region A(c), typically stabilized in the atlas as a canonical crystallization.

**Function in the theory.** Represents semantic individuation after coordinate-level structural generation.

**Distinguish from.** Not necessarily unique: more than one r may satisfy the same c.

**Relations.** See canonical crystallization; A(c); semantic individuation.

### manifestation m
**Preferred status:** Formal

**Definition.** A concrete event-instance m that realizes a generated form or canonical realization in particular semantic and often linguistic material.

**Function in the theory.** Represents the instance level of the architecture.

**Distinguish from.** Not the same as r: one canonical realization may have many manifestations.

**Relations.** See concrete manifestation; canonical realization r.

### decoder D
**Preferred status:** Formal

## XIV. Terminological discipline and ambiguity rules

### Opening / opening
Use **Opening** for the Transformation Pattern of enlarged availability. Use **opening** for the historical initial configuration of an event. Use **opening pressure** for the structured incompletion that makes further change necessary.

### form
Use **coordinate-generated form** for the structural result of a coordinate; **canonical crystallization** for the selected semantic realization; **concrete manifestation** for a particular instance; and **completed form** when speaking generically across these levels.

### grammar
Use **constraint grammar** or **coordinate grammar** for the theory-level compositional system. Do not imply ordinary linguistic grammar unless the object under analysis is specifically linguistic.

### geometry
Treat **geometry** as a secondary metaphor for state-space organization or topology. It is no longer the preferred name of the theory and should not replace **Completion Topology**.

### lattice
Use **lattice** for a representation of coordinate relations and periodic organization. Do not attribute generative force to the lattice itself; the generative principle is simultaneous constraint satisfaction in the constraint grammar.

### conjugation
Use **conjugation** for the structural analogy in which fixed coordinates are systematically modulated by substitutions along another axis, especially Standing → Bearing → Efficacy. Do not imply literal morphology.

### Standing
Use **Standing** for the persistence mode in which the achieved determination itself remains available. Do not equate it with physical stillness or permanence.

### Bearing
Use **Bearing** for the persistence mode in which the result remains through an indispensable relation. Do not confuse it with **Relating**, the Transformation Pattern by which a bearing becomes constitutive.

### Efficacy
Use **Efficacy** for persistence as achieved capacity, consequence, function, or changed continuation. Do not equate it with continuous activity or evaluative success.

### Release
Use **Release** only for completion by de-governance: something that previously determined the field loses its hold. Spatial movement away may instantiate Release but does not define it.

### primitive
Use **primitive** in the operational and architectural sense: a construct not presently decomposed inside this theory. Do not infer metaphysical irreducibility, innateness, or logical necessity.

### canonical
In this glossary, **canonical** means stabilized as the preferred reference realization in the mature atlas. It does not mean uniquely necessary, universally valid, or empirically privileged.

### complete / completion / exhaustive
**Complete** and **completion** are event-relative terms concerning sufficiency under a governing demand. **Exhaustive** is a theory-level claim that all possible forms are covered. The former is central to the theory; the latter remains unestablished.

## XVII. Compact final architecture

```text
T = {Differentiation, Opening, Relating, Repair, Integration, Settlement}
K = {Focal, Distributed, Release}
P = {Standing, Bearing, Efficacy}

C = T × K × P
|C| = 54

c = <t, k, p>
A(c) = admissible region satisfying the three constraints
G(c) = coordinate-generated phenomenological syntax
r ∈ A(c) = canonical realization / crystallization
m = concrete manifestation of r or another admissible realization

Transformation Pattern asks: What kind of unresolvedness changes?
Completion Topology asks: What makes that change sufficient?
Persistence Mode asks: In what mode does the achieved completion remain available?

coordinate-generated form ≠ canonical crystallization ≠ concrete manifestation
structural determination ≠ semantic individuation ≠ linguistic realization
```

The mature theory is therefore best stated as a **constraint grammar of completed intelligibility**: a finite, periodic, compositional architecture in which three operationally primitive dimensions jointly generate recognizable phenomenological syntaxes of completion, while leaving variable room for semantic crystallization and concrete realization.
