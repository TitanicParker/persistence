The Five-Year-Old Edition

How Does Something Become “Done Enough” to Make Sense?

This is the simplest version of the Constraint Grammar of Completed Intelligibility. The big idea is surprisingly small: when something is unfinished, it asks for something. When the right thing happens, the asking can stop.

1. Start with a puzzle that is still open

Imagine I put three cups on a table. Under one cup is a little red ball. I say, “Which cup has the ball?” You look at the cups. At first, you do not know. The question is still open.

Then I lift the middle cup. There is the red ball.

Something has changed. A moment ago, your mind was still waiting. Now it is not waiting in the same way. The middle cup has become the answer. You can point to it. The little puzzle has become complete enough for you to say, “That one.”

That change—from still waiting to now I can take this as the answer—is what this theory studies.

The shortest version: some situations are unfinished in a particular way. Then something happens that finishes the important part. Afterward, a result remains that we can use, understand, follow, choose, or act on.

“Complete” does not mean “finished forever.” You can find the ball and then start another game. You can finish building a bridge and later repair it. You can make a decision and later change your mind. The idea is only that one particular demand has been answered enough that it no longer controls what happens next.

2. An unfinished thing is not just “nothing”

Suppose a door is locked and you want to go outside. The problem is not a blank empty nothing. It is a very specific problem: you cannot get through the door yet.

That matters, because different unfinished situations need different kinds of answers.

If two crayons look almost the same and you need to know which is blue, you need a difference to become clear. If a gate is shut, you need a way through. If two train tracks are not connected, you need a connection. If a toy is broken, you need it fixed. If you have all the pieces of a toy but they are scattered, you need them put together. If you must choose chocolate or vanilla, you need the choosing to end.

The theory calls this special kind of unfinishedness opening pressure. That sounds difficult, but the idea is simple: the situation is leaning toward a certain kind of answer.

A locked door asks for access. A broken wheel asks for repair. A choice asks for a decision. A pile of pieces asks for organization. The shape of the problem helps tell us what kind of completion would count.

3. The first question: what changed?

The theory says there are six big kinds of change that keep appearing when things become complete enough to make sense.

1. Differentiation — “Now I can tell the difference.”

Two things were mixed up, unclear, or treated as the same. Then a difference becomes important. You sort red blocks from blue blocks. You point to the correct cup. You draw a line between “inside” and “outside.” Something becomes distinct.

2. Opening — “Now I can go farther.”

Something was blocked, closed, too small, or unavailable. Then more becomes possible. A door opens. A path appears. A new option becomes real. What could not continue before can continue now.

3. Relating — “Now these things belong together in a way that matters.”

Two or more things were separate. Then a connection, order, match, response, balance, or dependency becomes important. One key fits one lock. One event answers another. Two pieces line up. The relation becomes part of what the situation is.

4. Repair — “Now the thing that was wrong is no longer in charge.”

A wheel wobbles. A puzzle piece is upside down. A rule is being used incorrectly. Something does not fit the way it needs to. Repair changes the arrangement so the fault stops controlling the result.

5. Integration — “Now the pieces make a whole.”

A pile of Lego bricks is not yet a castle. A box of bicycle parts is not yet a bicycle. Integration happens when the pieces get roles inside something larger. The wheel becomes a wheel of the bicycle. The door becomes a door of the castle.

6. Settlement — “Now we do not have to keep deciding.”

There was still something left to choose, finish, explain, or continue. Then that demand ends. We choose vanilla. We reach the destination. We finish the game. The situation no longer asks for the same next step.

These six are called Transformation Patterns. They answer the first big question: What kind of unfinishedness changed?

4. The second question: what made the change enough?

Knowing what changed is not quite enough. We also need to know how the change became complete.

There are three main ways.

Focal

One special place, thing, answer, source, choice, or whole becomes the centre of the completion.

Distributed

The completion needs many places, parts, steps, cases, or connections working across a wider field.

Release

The completion happens because something that was controlling the problem stops controlling it.

Imagine a classroom trying to become quiet.

It could become quiet because the teacher points to one child who is still talking and that child stops. That is fairly focal: one place matters most.

It could become quiet because many children across the whole room gradually settle down together. That is distributed: the whole spread of the room matters.

Or perhaps everyone was noisy because a loud game was still running. The game ends, and the thing that was keeping the noise going is gone. That is like release: an old governor loses its hold.

The theory calls these three possibilities Completion Topologies. The word “topology” here does not mean ordinary shape like circle or square. It means the structural way the situation becomes sufficient.

5. The third question: what is left afterward?

After something becomes complete, what remains?

Again, the theory gives three answers.

Standing

“There it is.” The result itself now stands as something you can point to or take as settled.

Bearing

“It holds because of this.” The result remains through a relationship, support, contrast, condition, or connection.

Efficacy

“Now it works.” The result remains as an ability, effect, rule, power, or changed possibility.

Take a little bridge made from blocks.

If you finish it and simply say, “There is the bridge,” you are noticing its Standing.

If the bridge stays up because the two sides support each other, you are noticing its Bearing.

If the bridge now lets a toy car cross the gap, you are noticing its Efficacy.

These are called Persistence Modes. They answer the question: In what way does the completed result remain available?

6. Three questions make one little grammar

Now we can put the whole idea into three child-sized questions:

1. What changed?
2. What made that change enough?
3. What is left afterward?

The first question has six possible answers. The second has three. The third has three.

Six times three times three is fifty-four.

That gives the theory its 54 positions.

But the important thing is not memorizing fifty-four names. The important thing is that each position is made from the same three questions. It is like having six kinds of sandwich filling, three kinds of bread, and three ways to serve the sandwich. You do not need fifty-four unrelated recipes in your head. You understand the little system that makes them.

That is why this is called a constraint grammar rather than just a list. A list says, “Here are fifty-four things.” A grammar says, “Here are a few rules that can combine to tell us what sort of thing belongs here.”

7. Let us build one of the 54 forms

Imagine a toy robot that does not work because its battery is sitting the wrong way around.

What changed? The fault gets corrected. That is Repair.

What made the correction enough? The bad arrangement stops governing the robot. The battery is turned the right way, and the old wrong setup is no longer in charge. That is like Release.

What remains? The robot can move again. The important leftover is not just that the battery now looks correct. The robot has recovered an ability. That is Efficacy.

So this event can be described as:

Repair + Release + Efficacy: a fault stops governing the situation, and correct functioning becomes possible again.

Notice what we did. We did not begin with a special name and force the robot story to fit it. We began with the three questions. The coordinate itself helped us generate the kind of event that belongs there.

That is one of the most important claims in the whole theory.

8. The name is not the deepest thing

Suppose you see a cookie cutter shaped like a star. You can use it to make a star-shaped biscuit, a star-shaped piece of clay, or a star-shaped hole in paper.

The biscuit is not the cutter. The clay star is not the cutter. They are different things that share a shape.

The theory makes a similar distinction.

First comes the coordinate-generated form: the structural shape produced by the three questions.

Then there can be a canonical crystallization: a particularly clear, memorable way of naming or showing that shape.

Then there are concrete manifestations: real examples in language, machines, games, decisions, mathematics, organizations, stories, and other domains.

This matters because the theory is trying to discover reusable shapes of completion, not merely collect interesting examples.

9. A wrong answer can still be a complete answer

This sounds strange at first.

Imagine I ask, “Where is the red ball?” You confidently point to the left cup and say, “There!” But the ball is actually under the middle cup.

Your answer is wrong. But it is still understandable as an answer. It has enough shape for me to disagree with it.

The theory therefore separates intelligibility from truth.

Before we can ask whether something is true or false, right or wrong, good or bad, we usually need something definite enough to judge. A silly plan can still be a complete plan. A false story can still be a complete story. A mistaken explanation can still be clear enough for us to say exactly where it went wrong.

The theory studies that earlier question: How did this become definite enough to count as the thing we are now judging?

10. Things can finish in little pieces before the whole thing finishes

When you build a Lego castle, you might finish one tower while the rest of the castle is still unfinished.

The same thing happens in sentences, plans, machines, arguments, journeys, and games. A small part can become complete while the larger thing is still open.

The theory therefore distinguishes local completion from global completion.

A little relation can be settled inside a bigger repair. A small repair can happen while the whole project is really an integration. Many little things can happen, but one transformation may still govern how the whole event finally counts.

The theory calls that the governing transformation.

Then there is often a moment—or sometimes a stretch of time—when the whole event finally becomes readable as complete in its particular way. The theory calls that landing.

Landing is not one of the three coordinate questions. It belongs to the story of how we got there.

11. The map is useful because nearby places differ in simple ways

Imagine a board game with fifty-four squares. The squares are not random. Moving one step in one direction changes only one part of the rule.

That is useful because we can compare neighboring kinds of completion.

Keep the same change and the same way of becoming complete, but switch from Standing to Bearing. Now ask: instead of the result merely standing there, what relationship has to keep it what it is?

Switch from Bearing to Efficacy. Now ask: what can the completed thing do?

Or keep the same change but switch from Focal to Distributed. Now the completion cannot depend on only one special place; the wider field has to matter.

This repeating pattern is what the full theory calls periodicity. The 54 positions behave less like fifty-four unrelated boxes and more like variations made by a small set of repeating moves.

12. Why this might matter

If this map is a good one, it could help people notice something they often miss: being unfinished is not one single condition, and becoming complete is not one single event.

A teacher might ask what kind of unfinishedness a child is actually facing. Does the child need a distinction, an opening, a relation, a repair, an integration, or a settlement?

A programmer might ask why an automated agent keeps going when it should stop. Did it fail to recognize that a demand had been settled? Did it confuse a result that merely stands with a result that must actually work?

A team might ask why a project feels “done” to one person but unfinished to another. Are they using different completion conditions?

A scientist might ask whether apparently different examples share the same deeper form.

But those are possibilities for research, not proven consequences. The theory has built an internally coherent map. It has not yet proved that this map is the only possible map, that every human mind uses it, or that every event in every domain fits uniquely into one of the 54 positions.

13. What the theory knows, and what it does not know yet

The theory can already show something important inside its own structure.

The same three questions can be combined again and again. The 54 combinations produce recognizable event-shapes. The repeating pattern survives even when we remove the memorable names and describe only what must change, what makes the change sufficient, and what remains.

That is why the framework is more than a pile of examples.

But a beautiful map can still be incomplete.

Maybe there is a seventh kind of transformation. Maybe there is another way for completion to become sufficient. Maybe human beings disagree too much when they try to classify new cases. Maybe another, simpler theory explains the same things better.

Those are not embarrassments. They are tests.

A serious theory should tell us what could show that it needs to change.

14. The whole theory in one playground story

You and a friend want to play on the other side of a little stream.

At first, you cannot cross. The situation is open in a particular way: the other side is unavailable.

You find three flat stones and place them across the water.

What changed? Opening: a route became available.

What made it complete? Perhaps the whole span matters because all three stones are needed to cross. That is Distributed.

What remains afterward? The route is useful because it lets you get across. That is Efficacy.

So one simple playground event can be read as:

Opening + Distributed + Efficacy: access spreads across a necessary span until passage actually becomes possible.

Now change only one thing. Suppose the stones are placed, but nobody crosses yet. You simply look and say, “There is a path now.” That emphasizes Standing.

Or suppose the path only remains possible because each stone is placed at the right distance from the next. That emphasizes Bearing.

The event changes in a controlled way because one coordinate changes.

That is the little grammar at work.

15. The grown-up sentence hiding inside the child-sized idea

If you want the formal version, it is this:

Completed intelligibility can be described by combining a Transformation Pattern, a Completion Topology, and a Persistence Mode. Six transformations, three topologies, and three persistence modes produce a 54-position state-space.

But the child-sized version may actually be easier to remember:

What changed?
What made it enough?
What is left?

If those three questions keep working across many very different situations, then we may have found something real about how unfinished things become intelligible as completed things.

16. The smallest possible ending

A puzzle can still be asking.

Then something changes.

The change becomes enough.

Something remains.

And now we can say, “Ah. I see what happened.”

Constraint Grammar is an attempt to map the repeating shapes of that “Ah.”
