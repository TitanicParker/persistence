# The Constraint Grammar of Completed Intelligibility in the Academic Landscape

## Disciplinary Location, Explanatory Level, Novelty, and Research Programme

## Abstract

The Constraint Grammar of Completed Intelligibility proposes a finite, compositional architecture for recurrent ways in which a structured incompletion becomes sufficiently complete for determinate uptake. Its mature form distinguishes three independently recurring constraints: **Transformation Pattern**, which specifies what kind of change answers the opening pressure; **Completion Topology**, which specifies what makes that change sufficient as a completion; and **Persistence Mode**, which specifies the mode in which the achieved result remains available. Their product—six Transformation Patterns, three Completion Topologies, and three Persistence Modes—yields a 6 × 3 × 3 state-space of fifty-four coordinate positions. Each position generates a domain-general phenomenological event-syntax prior to any **canonical crystallization** or **concrete manifestation**.

This document asks where such a theory belongs academically. The answer cannot be obtained from vocabulary alone. “Grammar” does not make it linguistics; “phenomenological” does not make it classical phenomenology; “state-space” does not make it mathematics; “constraint” does not make it systems theory; “intelligibility” does not make it epistemology; and the possibility of computational implementation does not make it artificial intelligence. Disciplinary location must instead be earned by comparing the theory’s object, explanatory level, methods, formal status, empirical ambitions, and reduction risks with the corresponding commitments of candidate fields.

The strongest current placement is this: **the Constraint Grammar is best understood as a functional-phenomenological theory of completion structure situated primarily within theoretical cognitive science and the philosophy of cognitive science, with phenomenology as its closest descriptive neighbor**. Cognitive science is the best parent discipline because it is institutionally equipped to combine philosophical analysis, psychological testing, linguistic evidence, computational modeling, and artificial-intelligence implementation without requiring the theory to reduce completed intelligibility to any one of those domains. Philosophy of cognitive science is the best immediate intellectual home because the present framework specifies a level of organization and a candidate state-space rather than a validated mechanism. Phenomenology offers the closest methodological and descriptive affinity, but the finite Cartesian architecture, prospective coordinate generation, graded constraint widths, and cross-domain empirical ambitions exceed what can presently be justified as phenomenology alone.

The framework does not currently redefine linguistics, psychology, cognitive science, phenomenology, meaning, cognition, or academia. Its strongest defensible contribution is narrower and more exact: it proposes **completion structure as a distinct level of analysis**, separating what changes, what makes that change sufficient, and how the achieved result persists. If independently validated across linguistic and nonlinguistic materials, formally strengthened, and shown to predict novel classifications or behavioral regularities, it could become a recognizable subfield or research programme within cognitive science concerned specifically with the structural organization of intelligible completion. No independent discipline is yet warranted.

# 1. The Problem of Disciplinary Location

A mature theory should be located by the kind of object it studies, not by the route through which the object was discovered.

The Constraint Grammar was historically developed through sentences, local-to-global resolution, semantic heterogeneity, and repeated comparison among completed forms. But its mature object is not a sentence class. The theory’s own strongest claim is that semantically different materials can exhibit the same completed form, while semantically similar materials can diverge because they differ in transformation, completion topology, or persistence. The framework therefore treats sentence structure as one privileged observational surface rather than as the ontology of the system.

The proposed object is:

> **recurrent structural forms by which a structured incompletion becomes sufficiently complete for determinate uptake.**

This definition already rules out several easy disciplinary placements.

It is not primarily a theory of words, because the relevant invariants can survive lexical substitution and domain change.

It is not primarily a theory of sentence syntax, because the coordinate-generated form is not a constituent structure, dependency tree, phrase-structure description, or grammatical construction.

It is not primarily a theory of semantic content, because the same coordinate can be manifested by substantially different contents.

It is not primarily a theory of truth or inference, because intelligible completion can occur in false, fictional, hypothetical, mistaken, or normatively defective material.

It is not primarily a theory of cognitive mechanism, because the present architecture does not specify memory stores, neural circuits, activation dynamics, learning rules, or processing algorithms.

It is not primarily a theory of signs, because no sign relation is required by the formal statement of the three dimensions.

It is not primarily a theory of systems, because some manifestations are systemic while others are focal and local.

And it is not yet a mathematical theory in the strong sense, because its notation is a formal skeleton rather than an axiomatized calculus.

The disciplinary problem is therefore genuine. The theory appears to occupy a level that several established disciplines can approach but none obviously owns in advance.

The central question is not “Which field uses similar words?” It is:

> **Which field is prepared to treat completion structure itself as an object: a structured transition from unresolvedness to sufficient availability, decomposable into transformation, completion topology, and persistence?**

That question requires distinguishing disciplinary **home**, **parent**, **neighbor**, and **application/testing domain**.

A **home discipline** should recognize the primary object and explanatory level as substantially its own.

A **parent discipline** should provide the larger intellectual and institutional family in which the object can be investigated even if the framework is not reducible to the parent’s standard subfields.

A **neighbor discipline** should overlap significantly in concepts or methods without containing the object.

An **application/testing domain** may instantiate, test, operationalize, or exploit the grammar while remaining secondary to its disciplinary identity.

These relations are not mutually exclusive. Cognitive psychology, for example, can be both a neighboring field and a major testing domain. Linguistics can be both a historically privileged evidence source and an application domain. Philosophy can be an intellectual parent even if the long-term programme becomes empirically cognitive-scientific.

# 2. What Kind of Object Is Completed Intelligibility?

## 2.1 Not a content class but a completion structure

The theory is built around a distinction between what a situation is *about* and how it becomes *complete enough to be taken up*. A bridge across a ravine, a rule applying across cases, a corrected trajectory, a role filled in a system, and a retrospective explanation may have little semantic content in common. Yet each can instantiate a recurrent organization of unresolvedness, change, sufficiency, and residue.

The theory’s fundamental unit is therefore best characterized as a **structured transition**.

A completed form contains at least four analytically distinguishable moments:

1. an opening field with a determinate pressure;
2. a transformation that answers that pressure;
3. a condition under which the transformation becomes sufficient;
4. an achieved result that remains available in some mode.

The mature coordinate grammar abstracts the central three constraints from that historical movement:

**Transformation Pattern × Completion Topology × Persistence Mode**

The historical layer asks how the event unfolds. The coordinate layer asks what completed organization has been achieved.

This makes the framework process-sensitive without making it a general process ontology. It is about transitions of intelligibility, not the metaphysical thesis that reality itself is fundamentally processual.

## 2.2 The generated form is more fundamental than the canonical name

The theory’s most important internal refinement is the separation of three levels:

**coordinate-generated form → canonical crystallization → concrete manifestation**

The coordinate-generated form is what follows from the conjunction of the three structural constraints. It is expressed most clearly by a domain-free event-sentence.

The canonical crystallization is a more semantically specific realization that makes the generated region recognizable.

The concrete manifestation is an instance in a particular material, discourse, task, system, or experience.

This hierarchy matters for disciplinary placement. If the theory consisted only of fifty-four named categories, it would look like a taxonomy. If it consisted only of examples, it would look like descriptive phenomenology or cognitive semantics. Its stronger theoretical identity comes from the claim that the coordinates can prospectively generate a sentence-shaped event-form before the canonical crystallization is consulted.

The object is therefore not “the set of fifty-four labels.” It is a **constraint-generated space of completion structures**.

## 2.3 Intelligibility here is prior to truth but not identical with understanding

Contemporary epistemology and philosophy of science contain extensive debates about understanding, explanation, intelligibility, grasping, and epistemic value. The Stanford Encyclopedia’s survey of understanding emphasizes that understanding is treated as an epistemic achievement distinct in important ways from isolated knowledge, and contemporary philosophy of science has discussed intelligibility as a condition of scientific understanding (de Regt 2009, 2017). These are genuine neighboring discussions.

The Constraint Grammar uses *intelligibility* at a different level. It asks what makes a situation sufficiently organized to support determinate uptake before evaluating whether that uptake is epistemically successful. A false diagnosis, a fictional event, a mistaken plan, or a bad argument can be structurally complete enough to be understood as what it is. The framework therefore does not identify completed intelligibility with epistemically warranted understanding.

This is one reason epistemology is not the primary home. Epistemology can evaluate the norms governing successful understanding, but the Constraint Grammar proposes to describe the structural organization that makes a completed object available for such evaluation in the first place.

The relation is consequently:

**completed intelligibility** is a proposed structural precondition or organization of uptake;

**epistemic understanding** is a normatively assessable achievement involving a subject’s grasp of some object, relation, or explanation.

The first may contribute to the second without being equivalent to it.

# 3. What Explanatory Level Does the Theory Occupy?

Disciplinary confusion largely disappears once the explanatory level is stated correctly.

## 3.1 Direct explanatory target

The theory directly explains—or, more cautiously at its present stage, *systematizes*—the **form of completed availability**.

It asks:

- What kind of unresolvedness changed?
- What structural condition made that change sufficient?
- In what mode did the result remain available?

The three primitive dimensions answer those questions respectively.

The theory therefore operates primarily at a **structural-functional** level. “Structural” means that the form depends on relational organization rather than domain content. “Functional” means that the dimensions are individuated by the roles they play in moving a situation from unresolvedness to completed availability.

The adjective **phenomenological** is also justified, but in a qualified sense. The generated descriptions concern how a situation is experienced or taken up as unfinished, resolved, held, operative, or released. They are descriptions of recognizable forms of appearance and completion. But the theory is not limited to reports of first-person consciousness, and it does not yet employ the full methodological commitments of any particular historical phenomenological school.

The most accurate level description is therefore:

> **functional-phenomenological structural theory**

or, more compactly:

> **a theory of completion structure at the functional-phenomenological level.**

## 3.2 What the theory constrains but does not explain

The framework constrains candidate accounts of cognitive representation and processing insofar as those accounts would need to explain why the same completed forms recur across distinct manifestations if the atlas proves empirically valid.

But it does not presently explain:

- how a nervous system detects an opening pressure;
- how attention selects a focal completion;
- how memory stores a persistent bearing;
- how language encodes a generated form;
- how a learner acquires the dimensions;
- how probabilistic inference resolves ambiguity;
- how a neural network would implement coordinate classification;
- or how social institutions stabilize particular crystallizations.

Those are lower-level or domain-specific questions.

## 3.3 Relation to Marr-like levels

David Marr’s familiar distinction among computational, algorithmic/representational, and implementation levels is useful as a comparison, not an identity. Marr’s computational level specifies what mapping a system computes and why that mapping is appropriate; the algorithmic level concerns representations and procedures; the implementation level concerns physical realization.

The Constraint Grammar is closest to a **task-level or functional specification**. It proposes a structure of input-like incompletion and output-like completion without specifying the algorithms that produce the transition. But it is not yet a computational-level theory in Marr’s strict sense because the mappings are not mathematically specified, the relevant input/output spaces are not operationalized, and the adequacy conditions have not been empirically established.

Thus “Marr-like” is appropriate only to locate the abstraction level:

**above algorithm and mechanism; below unrestricted metaphysics; adjacent to functional description.**

## 3.4 What higher-level interpretation remains open

The framework also leaves open several philosophical interpretations.

It does not decide whether the three dimensions are metaphysically necessary.

It does not decide whether completed forms are mind-dependent.

It does not decide whether nonhuman animals instantiate the same forms.

It does not decide whether an artificial system can literally possess completed intelligibility or merely implement equivalent structural transitions.

It does not decide whether all cognition is organized through completion.

And it does not decide whether the atlas is exhaustive.

These questions belong to higher-level philosophical interpretation or future empirical research.

# 4. Criteria for Disciplinary Placement

A disciplinary home should be judged under eight criteria.

## 4.1 Object fit

Does the field treat structures of intelligible completion—or a sufficiently close object—as something worth studying in its own right?

## 4.2 Level fit

Does the field normally work at the level of functional organization and structured experience rather than requiring either linguistic form, neural mechanism, or metaphysical commitment?

## 4.3 Method fit

Can the field’s accepted methods develop, test, criticize, or refine the theory?

## 4.4 Vocabulary fit

Does the discipline already possess concepts capable of describing opening pressure, transformation, sufficiency, persistent result, generated form, and semantic realization without severe distortion?

## 4.5 Formal fit

Can the field naturally host a finite product state-space, constraint composition, graded admissible regions, and prospective generation?

## 4.6 Institutional fit

Would scholars in the field recognize the central questions as legitimate members of their research agenda?

## 4.7 Reduction risk

Would locating the theory in the field systematically collapse it into one manifestation—for example, language, perception, signification, or mechanism?

## 4.8 Expansion potential

Could the theory contribute a genuinely new level of analysis to the field if validated?

These criteria will be applied repeatedly below.

# 5. Philosophy

## 5.1 The strongest case for philosophy

The case for philosophy is substantial.

The theory asks about intelligibility prior to truth evaluation, about the conditions under which an unresolved field becomes sufficiently determinate for uptake, and about distinctions that are presented as operational primitives rather than discovered neural mechanisms. Those questions are recognizably philosophical.

The framework also separates conceptual levels that empirical sciences often presuppose: intelligibility versus truth, completion versus stopping, result state versus persistence mode, structure versus semantic individuation, and generated form versus manifestation. Such distinctions are characteristic work for philosophy of mind, philosophy of language, philosophy of cognition, and philosophy of science.

Philosophy is also equipped to evaluate the status of the primitive dimensions. Are they merely useful coordinates, psychologically real structures, transcendental conditions, or categories imposed by a model? Is the Cartesian product explanatory or representational? What sort of necessity is claimed? These are philosophical questions before they are experimental ones.

The contemporary philosophy of understanding offers a real neighboring literature. Work on understanding and intelligibility asks what it is to grasp an explanation or phenomenon and how intelligibility relates to scientific understanding. The overlap is not direct precedent for the Constraint Grammar, because that literature primarily concerns epistemic states and scientific understanding, but it demonstrates that “intelligibility” is already a philosophically legitimate object when carefully distinguished from subjective familiarity.

## 5.2 The case against philosophy as the whole home

The Constraint Grammar is not merely conceptual analysis.

Its mature architecture makes empirically vulnerable claims. It claims that three dimensions recur independently; that their 6 × 3 × 3 product yields a coherent state-space; that coordinate-generated sentences show nested periodicity independent of canonical naming; and that the same generated forms can recur across semantic domains.

Those claims invite classification studies, behavioral experiments, cross-linguistic tests, computational implementation, inter-rater reliability analysis, and out-of-atlas cases. A purely philosophical treatment would leave central ambitions unrealized.

The atlas is also not a transcendental deduction. Nothing in its current evidence proves that these forms are necessary conditions of possible experience or cognition. Calling the theory transcendental would therefore overstate its status.

Nor is it a metaphysics of intelligibility. The theory does not claim that reality itself is composed of fifty-four kinds of completion.

## 5.3 Proper relationship

**Judgment: Parent discipline and current intellectual home, especially philosophy of cognitive science; not sufficient as the eventual whole field.**

Within philosophy, the closest subareas are:

- philosophy of cognitive science;
- philosophy of mind;
- phenomenology;
- philosophy of language;
- philosophy of science, especially work on understanding and intelligibility.

Philosophy of cognitive science is particularly well placed because it can evaluate a proposed functional level without requiring premature commitment to a cognitive mechanism.

# 6. Phenomenology

## 6.1 The strongest case for phenomenology

The descriptive vocabulary of the Constraint Grammar is unmistakably phenomenological in a broad sense.

The theory begins from how unresolvedness is experienced or taken up: something is missing, unsettled, unorganized, restricted, misfitted, or still demanding further uptake. It describes a decisive transformation and asks what makes the situation feel or count as complete. Its persistence modes can be rendered in ordinary experiential compressions:

**Standing:** there it is.

**Bearing:** it holds through this.

**Efficacy:** now it works or takes effect.

Release is described not as spatial departure but as the loss of a prior governor’s hold. Retrospective settlement reorganizes what earlier events now mean. Companion utterances function as felt compressions of completed situations.

Phenomenology, in its broad disciplinary sense, studies structures of experience and the ways things appear or are intended in experience. The Stanford Encyclopedia describes phenomenology as the study of structures of conscious experience, typically from the first-person point of view, including temporal, spatial, attentional, bodily, social, and linguistic experience. That makes phenomenology a strong methodological neighbor.

The atlas’s practice of moving among diverse examples while abstracting the invariant structure also bears a family resemblance to phenomenological variation: one varies incidental content to isolate what remains structurally necessary for a type of experience. That resemblance is methodological, not a claim of direct Husserlian ancestry.

## 6.2 Why phenomenology is not sufficient

Three features push the theory beyond phenomenology as usually delimited.

First, the theory does not make first-person consciousness constitutive of its object. A bridge, a mechanical support system, or a case rule can instantiate a form in the atlas as long as the situation is being analyzed as an intelligible completion. The framework may ultimately require an interpreting subject, but it has not built subjectivity into the formal coordinates.

Second, the finite 6 × 3 × 3 architecture is a stronger formal claim than ordinary phenomenological description. It says not merely that these structures can be described, but that they arise compositionally from three axes and display nested periodicity.

Third, the theory has prospective generative ambitions. Given a coordinate triple, one can derive a domain-free event-syntax before consulting the canonical crystallization. This moves from description toward formal generation and empirical prediction.

## 6.3 Is “phenomenological syntax” appropriately named?

Yes, if the adjective is understood carefully.

“Syntax” does not mean linguistic syntax. It means an ordered functional organization of event roles: transformation-pattern, completion-pattern, persistence-pattern.

“Phenomenological” indicates that the output describes the recognizable form in which a situation becomes complete for uptake.

The term should not be taken to imply adherence to a historical phenomenological method or school.

## 6.4 Proper relationship

**Judgment: Strongest descriptive neighbor; possible co-home for conceptual development; insufficient as sole disciplinary container.**

A suitable characterization would be **formal or generative phenomenology of completion**, but only as a descriptive label, not yet as an established subfield.

# 7. Linguistics

## 7.1 The strongest case for linguistics

Linguistics must be taken seriously because language supplied a privileged route into the theory and because the mature framework uses terms such as grammar, syntax, compositionality, local-to-global organization, and generated form.

Several linguistic traditions are especially relevant.

Formal semantics studies systematic relations between linguistic syntax and interpretation. Montague semantics, for example, treats the meaning of a whole expression as determined compositionally by the meanings of its parts and their syntactic mode of combination.

Construction grammar treats grammatical knowledge in terms of constructions and organized relations among utterance structures.

Cognitive linguistics explicitly links linguistic structure to conceptualization. Croft and Cruse characterize cognitive linguistics through claims that language is not an autonomous cognitive faculty, grammar is conceptualization, and linguistic knowledge emerges through use. Cognitive linguistic work on construal is especially relevant because it studies how the same situation can be structured conceptually in different ways.

Event semantics and aspect provide further nearby concepts. Linguistic event structure analyzes how clauses represent states, processes, changes, subevents, telicity, and temporal constitution.

These literatures make language an excellent place to test whether coordinate-generated completion structure predicts differences among constructions or interpretations that existing levels do not capture.

## 7.2 Why the Constraint Grammar is not a linguistic grammar

A linguistic grammar specifies systematic knowledge of a language: phonological, morphological, syntactic, semantic, constructional, or usage-based regularities governing linguistic expressions.

The Constraint Grammar does not presently specify any such system.

Its primitives are not linguistic categories.

A coordinate does not determine a word order, syntactic dependency, morphological marking, argument structure, construction, truth condition, or discourse move.

Different languages could in principle realize the same coordinate through radically different forms.

Different constructions within one language could realize the same coordinate.

Conversely, one grammatical construction could potentially participate in different coordinates depending on what transformation becomes governing and what completion persists.

Therefore **grammar** in the mature theory is a nonlinguistic theoretical use: a finite set of independently recombinable constraints that generates structured outputs. It is analogous to grammar in its systematicity and productivity, not identical to grammatical competence in the linguistic sense.

## 7.3 Does phenomenological syntax lie beneath linguistic syntax?

“Beneath” is too strong if it means that linguistic syntax is derived from the coordinate grammar.

The current theory has not shown that phrase structure, dependency, or constructional organization is generated by completion coordinates.

A safer relation is:

> linguistic structures may **realize** or **cue** completion structures.

This is a cross-level realization hypothesis.

If validated, completion structure could become a new level of linguistic analysis located between conceptual/event organization and discourse/pragmatic uptake. It might help explain why semantically and syntactically different sentences can converge on the same form of completed intelligibility.

But that remains a research hypothesis.

## 7.4 Does the theory redefine linguistics, syntax, or grammar?

It does not redefine linguistics.

It does not redefine syntax.

It does not displace semantic or pragmatic theory.

It does introduce a principled nonlinguistic use of **grammar**: constraint composition that generates completion forms. This is a legitimate theoretical extension of the word, but it should always be distinguished from linguistic grammar.

The potentially important contribution to linguistics is **completion structure as an additional level of analysis**.

## 7.5 Proper relationship

**Judgment: Major neighbor, historically privileged evidence source, and primary testing domain; not the home discipline.**

Reduction risk is high. If the theory is placed inside linguistics too early, nonlinguistic manifestations will be treated as metaphorical extensions of sentence structure even though the mature theory makes the opposite claim: sentence structure is one realization domain of a more general completion grammar.

# 8. Cognitive Psychology

## 8.1 The strongest case for cognitive psychology

Completed intelligibility plainly involves phenomena cognitive psychology already studies:

- categorization;
- attention;
- problem solving;
- decision making;
- comprehension;
- prediction;
- cognitive control;
- event cognition;
- relational reasoning;
- schema formation;
- mental models;
- Gestalt organization;
- closure.

Research on event cognition is particularly relevant. Contemporary event-perception research investigates how observers segment continuous activity into events, update event models, remember event structure, and use boundaries during comprehension. Such work supplies mechanisms and experimental paradigms that could test the historical-formation layer of the Constraint Grammar.

Gestalt psychology is another real neighbor. Gestalt approaches study the organization of experience into coherent wholes and include perceptual closure as one principle by which incomplete forms are perceived as complete.

The Constraint Grammar’s Integration family, local-to-global organization, and concern with sufficiency resonate with these traditions.

## 8.2 Why completed intelligibility is not simply psychological closure

The term **closure** is dangerously broad.

Gestalt closure concerns perceptual organization in which missing elements are supplied or incomplete forms are perceived as wholes.

Everyday psychological closure can mean a sense of resolution.

Completed intelligibility is more general and more differentiated. It includes cases where nothing perceptually incomplete is filled in, no emotional issue is resolved, and no missing visual contour is completed.

A focal distinction, distributed reach, conditional repair, role activation, or explanatory bearing can be complete without resembling Gestalt closure.

Thus Gestalt theory supplies an important precedent for treating completion and organization as psychologically real, but not a direct ancestor of the 6 × 3 × 3 grammar.

## 8.3 Functional structure versus psychological mechanism

The current theory does not posit a psychological mechanism.

It does not say whether coordinates are represented explicitly.

It does not specify whether classification is serial or parallel.

It does not specify whether opening pressure is predictive error, attentional demand, working-memory tension, uncertainty, or something else.

It does not specify learning, development, or individual differences.

Therefore the best psychological interpretation is:

> **a functional-level hypothesis about the structural products or organizations that cognitive mechanisms may achieve.**

Psychology would be one of the strongest fields for testing the theory, but it should not yet be described as a psychological mechanism theory.

## 8.4 Proper relationship

**Judgment: Major testing discipline and conceptual neighbor; not yet the home discipline.**

If behavioral experiments showed reliable coordinate discrimination, systematic confusions among neighboring cells, processing signatures corresponding to topology or persistence, or transfer across domains, cognitive psychology could become a stronger home.

# 9. Cognitive Science

## 9.1 The strongest case

Cognitive science is designed precisely for problems whose explanation may require coordinated philosophical, psychological, linguistic, computational, neuroscientific, and artificial-intelligence perspectives.

The Cognitive Science Society explicitly describes the field as drawing together multiple empirical methods, formal tools, and analytic theories in the study of minds and intelligent systems. Its conference scope includes psychology, linguistics, philosophy, AI, computational systems, logic, machine learning, education, anthropology, neuroscience, and robotics.

That institutional breadth matters because the Constraint Grammar presently has:

- a philosophical object definition;
- phenomenological descriptive evidence;
- a linguistic discovery and testing route;
- psychological hypotheses;
- a formal state-space skeleton;
- potential computational implementation;
- and possible AI applications.

No narrower empirical discipline can host all of these without prematurely deciding what the theory reduces to.

## 9.2 The exact fit

The theory fits cognitive science best at a **functional/structural level**.

It does not claim to model neural implementation.

It does not yet provide an algorithm.

It does not specify a learning theory.

It proposes a structured space of completed cognitive or intelligible products and a set of constraints that may organize transitions into those products.

This resembles the role that some cognitive-scientific theories play before full mechanism is known: they define a task space, a representational hypothesis, or a functional architecture that subsequent models must implement or falsify.

The important qualification is that the Constraint Grammar should not yet be called a **cognitive architecture** in the standard strong sense. Cognitive architectures usually specify processing components, representational formats, control structures, memory systems, or algorithms capable of generating behavior. The present theory specifies none of these.

It is better described as a **candidate completion architecture** at the functional level.

## 9.3 Does it redefine cognitive science?

No.

The strongest claim is that it may add a **new cross-domain level of analysis** within cognitive science: completion structure.

That is level 2 in a conservative scale of disciplinary change: adding a new analytic level, not revising the foundations of the discipline.

If the three dimensions prove experimentally robust and computationally useful, the framework could become a research programme spanning event cognition, language comprehension, reasoning, planning, learning, and AI.

## 9.4 Proper relationship

**Judgment: Best parent discipline and best long-term institutional home.**

Cognitive science has the highest combined object, level, method, formal, and institutional fit while imposing the lowest reduction risk.

# 10. Philosophy of Mind, Philosophy of Language, and Philosophy of Cognition

These areas deserve separate treatment from philosophy in general because they are plausible immediate homes for an initial paper.

## 10.1 Philosophy of mind

The theory concerns structures that may characterize mental uptake, but it does not address consciousness, representation, intentionality, or mental causation directly enough to count as a philosophy-of-mind theory in the narrow sense.

Phenomenal intentionality theories, for example, concern the relation between conscious phenomenology and representational content. The Constraint Grammar does not claim that completed intelligibility grounds intentionality or that its forms are phenomenal contents.

**Judgment: neighbor and intellectual context, not primary object fit.**

## 10.2 Philosophy of language

The framework can clarify a distinction between linguistic form, semantic content, and completion structure. It may therefore contribute to debates about compositionality, event structure, context, and the relation between sentence meaning and understanding.

But it is not presently a theory of reference, truth conditions, speech acts, or linguistic meaning.

**Judgment: neighbor and application domain.**

## 10.3 Philosophy of cognition

This is the strongest philosophical subfield fit.

The theory asks what kind of organization cognition achieves, at what level such organization should be described, how structural and semantic determination differ, and how a functional architecture might relate to mechanism.

**Judgment: best immediate intellectual home.**

If a conceptual paper had to be submitted before the empirical programme existed, philosophy of cognitive science would be the most defensible first audience.

# 11. Semiotics

## 11.1 The strongest case

Semiotics studies signification, representation, interpretation, and relations among signs, objects, and interpretants. Peirce’s semiotic is especially broad and treats interpretation as integral to signification.

There is an obvious family resemblance to the Constraint Grammar’s interest in how materials become intelligibly organized for uptake.

Canonical crystallizations can be linguistically or symbolically expressed.

Companion utterances are signs that compress completed forms.

Retrospective stabilization can reorganize interpretation.

The distinction between generated structure and manifestation also resembles the semiotic distinction between abstract relations and concrete sign vehicles.

## 11.2 Why semiotics is not the home

A sign relation is not primitive in the coordinate grammar.

Differentiation, Opening, Repair, Integration, and Settlement can be instantiated in nonsemiotic materials.

The generated form does not require a sign vehicle, object, and interpretant.

The theory therefore concerns a broader organization of completion in which semiosis can participate.

**Judgment: significant neighbor, especially for linguistic and symbolic manifestations; not a containing discipline.**

The relation to Peircean semiotics should be classified as **structural resemblance and useful comparison**, not established lineage.

# 12. Formal Semantics and Logic

## 12.1 The strongest case

The Constraint Grammar is compositional.

A coordinate is defined by the conjunction of three constraints.

The state-space is a Cartesian product.

Coordinate substitution produces controlled contrasts.

Generated syntax can be treated as a function of coordinates.

These features invite logical and formal-semantic modeling.

Formal semantics also provides mature methods for distinguishing semantic types, compositional rules, model-theoretic interpretation, scope, and event structure.

## 12.2 Why it is not yet a formal semantic system

The current notation does not assign truth conditions.

It does not specify a model class.

It does not define an interpretation function over a formal language.

It does not provide proof rules, inference rules, algebraic operations, or completeness/soundness results.

“Intersection” is presently a conceptual statement of simultaneous constraint satisfaction, not a mathematically defined operation over fully specified sets.

The 54-state product is mathematical representation, but the theory as a whole is not yet mathematical in the strong sense.

## 12.3 What genuine formalization would require

At minimum:

1. explicit domains for situations, opening pressures, transformations, topologies, and persistence modes;
2. operational criteria for membership in each primitive;
3. a formal definition of admissible region \(A(c)\);
4. a generation function \(G(c)\) with a specified output space;
5. a representation of graded width or constraint strength;
6. transition relations for historical formation;
7. a mapping from manifestations to coordinates;
8. explicit conditions for ambiguity and out-of-atlas cases;
9. predictions that discriminate the formal model from alternatives.

Formal semantics could contribute tools, but the target is broader than linguistic meaning.

**Judgment: formal neighbor and likely formalization resource, not a home discipline.**

# 13. Systems Theory

## 13.1 The strongest case

Systems theory studies organization, interdependence, feedback, constraint, coordination, whole-part relations, and state transitions across domains. General systems traditions explicitly sought common organizational principles across superficially different systems.

The Constraint Grammar shares this cross-domain structural ambition.

Integration, distributed completion, reciprocal bearing, emergence, equilibrium, and release from governance are all naturally expressible in systems language.

The use of **constraint** also resonates with systems and organizational theories in which constraints channel what a system can do.

## 13.2 Why systems theory is insufficient

The Constraint Grammar does not begin with systems as its object.

Many forms involve one focal item, one boundary, one option, one relation, or one settlement. Calling every completed situation a “system” would make the term so broad that it ceases to discriminate.

More importantly, systems theory typically studies the organization and dynamics of systems themselves. The Constraint Grammar studies the **form in which a situation becomes intelligibly complete**. That is a different explanatory target.

Systems language can redescribe many atlas cells without explaining why the three axes should be the primitive dimensions of completion.

**Judgment: strong structural neighbor and formal-resource discipline; not the home.**

# 14. Complexity Science

## 14.1 Genuine overlaps

Complexity science studies interactions among many components, self-organization, emergent macro-patterns, adaptation, networks, and cross-scale dynamics. The Santa Fe Institute characterizes complex systems science in terms of shared mechanisms across physical, biological, social, and technological systems, often emphasizing emergence from interacting agents.

Several atlas forms—especially Relating/Distributed/Efficacy and Integration/Distributed or Release forms—fit naturally within complexity-scientific examples.

Local-to-global emergence and distributed organization are genuine points of contact.

## 14.2 Limits of the comparison

Most of the 54 forms do not require many interacting agents, nonlinearity, adaptation, emergence, or complex dynamics.

Terms such as attractor, phase transition, criticality, and self-organization should therefore not be imported without formal evidence.

The fact that the atlas is a state-space does not make it a dynamical system.

**Judgment: application/testing domain for a subset of forms and a source of formal methods; not a disciplinary home.**

# 15. Information Theory

## 15.1 The tempting reduction

It is easy to redescribe completed intelligibility as uncertainty reduction.

An unresolved situation appears uncertain.

Differentiation reduces ambiguity.

Settlement closes alternatives.

A completed result can look like information gained.

This resemblance is real enough to merit testing.

## 15.2 Why the reduction fails at present

Shannon information theory is a mathematical theory of probabilistic uncertainty, coding, transmission, storage, and channel capacity. Its central quantities are entropy, mutual information, and related measures.

The Constraint Grammar does not assign probabilities to alternatives.

It does not measure bits.

It does not define a source distribution or channel.

More importantly, several coordinate distinctions are orthogonal to uncertainty reduction. Standing, Bearing, and Efficacy can occur with equal informational uncertainty. Focal, Distributed, and Release describe different conditions of sufficiency, not different entropy values. Integration may increase structural dependence without reducing uncertainty in any Shannon sense.

Shannon himself sharply separated the engineering measure of information from semantic meaning. Later overextensions of information-theoretic vocabulary provide a cautionary precedent.

A formal information-theoretic interpretation could eventually be useful for particular tasks, but no identity has been established.

**Judgment: superficial-to-useful analogy depending on application; not a home, and reduction to entropy would currently be a category mistake.**

# 16. Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning

## 16.1 What the theory could become for AI

If validated, the grammar could support several AI uses:

- a structured ontology for classifying completion events;
- an interpretability vocabulary for describing what kind of resolution a model has produced;
- a symbolic scaffold for planning or reasoning;
- a control representation for generation;
- a latent-label scheme for training or evaluating models;
- a prompt or design language for constructing outputs with specified completion forms;
- an intermediate representation for comparing semantically different tasks.

The distinction between generated form and canonical manifestation is especially attractive for representation learning: a system could in principle learn invariant completion structure across heterogeneous surface domains.

## 16.2 What it is not yet

It is not a machine-learning model.

It has no training objective.

It has no parameterization.

It has no demonstrated latent correspondence in neural networks.

It is not an AI cognitive architecture.

It is not an interpretability method until it yields reliable explanatory or predictive benefits for actual models.

Application potential must not be mistaken for disciplinary identity.

**Judgment: major future application and implementation domain; not the current home.**

# 17. Computational Cognitive Modeling

Computational cognitive modeling is a more serious candidate than AI application because it could convert the functional architecture into executable hypotheses.

The present theory already supplies candidate **states or state labels**, a product structure, and constraints on outputs.

What it lacks is the machinery that would make the model run.

Required additions include:

- transition rules;
- temporal dynamics;
- a representation of opening pressure;
- algorithms for coordinate selection;
- uncertainty handling;
- learning or adaptation;
- memory;

A computational model could ask whether the three dimensions are recovered as separable latent factors, whether human confusion matrices follow coordinate distance, or whether generated forms predict processing costs.

# 18. Knowledge Representation and Ontology Engineering

## 18.1 The case for ontology

In computer and information science, ontologies explicitly specify concepts and relations in a domain. Gruber’s influential formulation describes ontology as a specification of a conceptualization, and knowledge-engineering practice uses ontologies to make domain commitments explicit.

The 54-state atlas is structured, finite, and machine-readable in principle. It could therefore be encoded as an ontology.

## 18.2 Why “ontology” misstates the theory if used as the primary label

The grammar does not primarily classify entities.

It does not even primarily classify events after they occur.

It generates admissible completion forms from dimensions.

The important relation is compositional constraint, not taxonomic subsumption.

An ontology can represent the grammar, but representation should not be confused with theoretical identity.

**Judgment: representational technology and application domain, not the home discipline.**

# 19. Structuralism

A broad structuralist affinity is genuine.

The identity of a local form depends on its position in a structured field of contrasts.

Holding one axis constant while varying another reveals invariants.

A canonical crystallization is partly intelligible through neighboring coordinates.

The same content can receive different structural descriptions depending on relational position.

These are structuralist habits of thought.

But “Structuralism” also names historically specific intellectual movements with claims about language, culture, signs, and social structure. No direct historical descent has been demonstrated.

The Constraint Grammar also differs from static structuralism by making transformation and completion central. Its primitive unit is a structured transition, not merely a position in a synchronic system.

**Judgment: broad methodological resemblance; not a disciplinary home and not an established historical lineage.**

# 20. Process Philosophy and Process Theory

The theory is explicitly transition-centered.

Opening pressure is answered through transformation.

Completion is sufficiency relative to a demand.

Historical formation matters.

These features make process approaches relevant.

Process philosophy, however, is typically a metaphysical project claiming that becoming, activity, or process is fundamental to reality. The Constraint Grammar makes no such ontological claim.

Its local objects are best described as **structured transitions connecting an unresolved field to a mode of completed availability**. They are neither mere processes nor mere terminal states.

**Judgment: conceptual neighbor; metaphysical process philosophy would overstate the theory’s commitments.**

# 21. Mathematics, Algebra, and Category-Theoretic Possibilities

## 21.1 What is genuinely mathematical now

The architecture contains a literal Cartesian product:

T \times K \times P

with cardinality:

The indexing exhibits nested periodicity.

Coordinate substitution can be represented formally.

These are real mathematical representations.

## 21.2 What is not yet mathematical

There is no theorem showing that the primitives are independent.

There is no metric on the state-space.

There is no formal topology despite the theoretical use of “Completion Topology.”

There is no lattice in the order-theoretic sense unless an ordering relation with meets and joins is defined.

There is no algebra of composition among completed forms.

There is no proof of closure, completeness, minimality, or exhaustiveness.

Therefore **periodic lattice** is presently a representational metaphor for regular product structure, not a claim that the atlas is a mathematical lattice in the strict sense.

Transformations, mappings, composition, and invariants make category-theoretic representation imaginable.

Invoking category theory now would add prestige vocabulary rather than explanatory content.

**Judgment: future formal option only if a concrete categorical advantage is demonstrated.**

# 24. Gestalt Psychology and the Concept of Closure

Gestalt psychology deserves separate treatment because “completion” invites an easy but misleading assimilation.

Gestalt psychology emphasizes organized wholes and the perception of configurations rather than atomistic sensations. Perceptual closure is one principle by which incomplete sensory patterns are perceived as complete.

The Constraint Grammar shares three broad insights:

1. organization can be irreducible to isolated parts;
2. completion can be a real feature of experience;
3. whole-structure can determine how parts count.

But its notion of completion is much broader.

Completion can be focal, distributed, or release-based.

Its residue can stand, bear, or become efficacious.

It includes decision, correction, relation, integration, availability, explanation, and obligation.

The strongest relation is therefore **partial conceptual precedent** for organization and completion, not identity.

The theory does not redefine Gestalt closure. It generalizes the question of completion into a wider structural space.

# 27. Novelty Analysis

The novelty question should be divided into components rather than answered with a single adjective.

## 27.1 Identifying completed intelligibility as an independent object

**Status: plausibly distinctive; requires broader literature verification and empirical validation.**

Many disciplines study understanding, closure, comprehension, organization, event boundaries, meaning, and resolution. The potentially distinctive move is treating the *structural form of becoming sufficiently complete for uptake* as an object independent of subject matter and linguistic realization.

The internal framework clearly makes this move. Whether it is historically unprecedented requires more extensive literature review.

## 27.2 Separating transformation, completion topology, and persistence

**Status: clearly supported internally; plausibly distinctive externally.**

The atlas supplies systematic substitutions showing that each dimension can recur while the others change. The conceptual distinction is one of the strongest contributions of the theory.

External distinctiveness is plausible because neighboring disciplines usually separate other dimensions—syntax/semantics, perception/action, process/result, figure/ground, input/output—but no reviewed framework uses this exact tripartition for completed intelligibility.

## 27.3 Treating the dimensions as independently recurring constraints

**Status: clearly supported by internal construction; requires empirical validation as a cognitive claim.**

Combinatorial independence in the atlas is explicit.

Psychological independence, cultural invariance, or ontological independence has not been shown.

## 27.4 Cartesian composition into fifty-four positions

**Status: clearly supported as formal construction; empirical significance unvalidated.**

The 54 positions follow directly from 6 × 3 × 3.

What remains open is whether all positions correspond to recurrent natural forms and whether relevant forms lie outside the product.

## 27.5 Periodic generated syntax

**Status: clearly supported internally and theoretically important.**

This is one of the strongest nontrivial features of the mature theory.

The periodicity is not merely in labels or numbering. The coordinate-generated sentences repeat functional roles at nested frequencies:

- transformation changes every nine positions;
- topology every three;
- persistence every position.

This is what licenses describing the atlas as a conjugation-like grammar rather than a list.

External novelty remains to be established through literature review and independent generation tasks.

## 27.6 Standing / Bearing / Efficacy

**Status: plausibly distinctive and central; empirical status open.**

The distinction has a strong phenomenological intuition:

there it is / it holds through this / now it works.

Its value is that it separates achieved presence, constitutive relational persistence, and accomplished consequence.

No surveyed neighboring framework offers this exact triad as a general grammar of post-completion availability.

## 27.7 Release as de-governance

**Status: plausibly distinctive formulation.**

Release is not motion away. It is completion because a prior determinant loses governance.

This avoids a spatial metaphor and unifies otherwise different forms such as released options, corrected faults, equilibrium, and retrospective settlement.

The concept resembles constraint removal in systems and cognitive theories but its role as one of three universal completion topologies is specific to this framework.

## 27.8 Graded width of admissible regions

**Status: clearly supported by the atlas methodology; conceptually important.**

The distinction among Near-determinate, Tightly constrained, and Open realization prevents the theory from confusing structural generation with semantic naming.

It also turns apparent unevenness into a theoretical property: coordinates differ in how strongly they determine local semantic realization.

Empirical measurement of width remains to be developed.

## 27.9 Generated form versus canonical crystallization

**Status: clearly supported internally; methodologically significant.**

This distinction is essential to preventing circularity.

If a canonical name were treated as the coordinate itself, prospective generation would be impossible to test.

The generated-form/crystallization distinction creates a principled place for semantic freedom.

## 27.10 Historical formation versus completed form

**Status: clearly supported conceptually; empirical process claims remain open.**

The theory correctly distinguishes the route by which a form becomes complete from the completed coordinate itself.

This permits path variation, translation, reanalysis, and multiple realization without changing the final structural form.

## 27.11 Prospective generation from coordinates

**Status: internally demonstrated; requires independent replication.**

Prospective generation is the strongest evidence that the architecture is grammatical rather than merely classificatory.

However, because the theory’s developers and models have been exposed to the atlas, truly independent blinded generation is still required.

## 27.12 Claims that are too strong at present

The following would exceed the evidence:

- “the 54 forms are all possible forms of intelligibility”;
- “the grammar is a universal law of cognition”;
- “the axes are metaphysically necessary”;
- “the system is a new mathematics of meaning”;
- “the atlas is a universal grammar beneath language”;
- “the theory proves a new cognitive architecture”;
- “the framework establishes a new academic discipline”;
- “the theory redefines academia.”

# 28. What the Theory Actually Redefines

The word **redefine** should be used sparingly.

## 28.1 Grammar

The framework does not redefine linguistic grammar.

It establishes a broader theoretical use of *grammar* meaning a finite system of recombinable constraints that generates structured forms.

**Judgment: extends a theoretical metaphor with explicit compositional content; does not revise linguistics’ definition of grammar.**

## 28.2 Syntax

“Phenomenological syntax” names ordered functional roles in completion events.

It does not redefine linguistic syntax.

**Judgment: new analytic usage, not disciplinary redefinition.**

## 28.3 Meaning

The theory separates completed intelligibility from semantic content.

It therefore proposes that meaning and completion structure are analytically distinguishable.

It does not redefine meaning itself.

**Judgment: adds a neighboring level of analysis.**

## 28.4 Completion

Here the theory does make a substantive conceptual proposal.

Completion is not mere stopping, saturation, perceptual closure, or endpoint arrival. It is **sufficiency relative to a governing opening pressure**, and that sufficiency can be focal, distributed, or release-based.

**Judgment: genuine theoretical reframing of completion within the framework.**

## 28.5 Intelligibility

The theory proposes a specific structural sense of intelligibility: sufficient organization for determinate uptake prior to truth evaluation.

This is narrower than everyday “understandability” and different from epistemically successful understanding.

**Judgment: introduces a disciplined technical sense rather than replacing existing philosophical uses.**

## 28.6 Cognition

The theory does not redefine cognition.

At most, it proposes that one underdescribed dimension of cognitive products may be completion structure.

**Judgment: possible new level of cognitive analysis, pending validation.**

## 28.7 Linguistics, psychology, cognitive science, phenomenology

None is redefined.

The theory could extend each by supplying a shared completion-structure vocabulary if the architecture generalizes.

That is a level-2 or level-3 contribution: adding an analytic level or reframing a problem, not revising disciplinary foundations.

## 28.8 Academia

No.

“Redefining academia” would require evidence that the framework reorganizes disciplinary boundaries, methods, institutional structures, or knowledge production at large.

The current theory does none of these.

At most, it identifies an object that falls across existing boundaries and therefore motivates cross-disciplinary coordination.

# 29. Is a New Field Warranted?

## 29.1 The case for a new field

There is a serious argument.

The proposed object—structural forms of completed intelligibility as such—is not clearly owned by any existing discipline.

Linguistics studies language.

Psychology studies cognitive mechanisms and behavior.

Phenomenology studies structures of experience.

Epistemology studies knowledge and understanding.

Semiotics studies signification.

Systems theory studies organized systems.

AI studies artificial intelligent systems.

The Constraint Grammar’s object cuts across all of these while remaining reducible to none.

If repeated empirical work showed that the same completion forms recur across perception, language, reasoning, planning, social coordination, education, and artificial systems, then a dedicated research programme would be justified.

## 29.2 Why an independent field is premature

A discipline requires more than a distinctive vocabulary and internal architecture.

It normally requires:

- a stable empirical object;
- reproducible methods;
- a community of investigators;
- cumulative findings;
- competing theories;
- specialized training;
- canonical problems;
- dedicated publication venues or conference tracks;
- and evidence that existing disciplines systematically cannot house the work.

The Constraint Grammar currently lacks most of these institutional and empirical conditions.

Cognitive science is already explicitly designed to host cross-domain theories of mind and intelligence. Creating a new field before testing whether cognitive science can contain the programme would be unjustified.

## 29.3 Strongest warranted description

The strongest current identity is:

> **a theoretical research programme in cognitive science and philosophy of cognition, centered on a proposed functional-phenomenological level of analysis called completion structure.**

It is interdisciplinary in method and evidence, but its identity is not merely “interdisciplinary.” The object is specific enough to be theoretically unified.

It is not transdisciplinary in the strong sense of superseding disciplinary categories.

It is not yet pre-disciplinary in the sense of an established future science awaiting institutionalization.

It is a **candidate subfield-level research programme**.

## 29.4 Candidate future identity

If independent empirical and formal validation succeeds, a subfield could emerge around the study of **completion structure**.

The most defensible future names would be modest and object-based, for example:

- **Completion Structure Research**
- **Cognitive Completion Theory**
- **Formal Phenomenology of Completion**

Of these, **Completion Structure Research** is least committal about mechanism and philosophical lineage.

A separate discipline would only become warranted if the object proves robust across domains and if no existing cognitive-science infrastructure can adequately coordinate the programme.

# 30. What Would Count as Progress?

A research programme requires explicit criteria.

## 30.1 Empirical progress

Progress would include:

- independent annotators recovering coordinates reliably;
- novel examples being classified above chance without canonical names;
- blind prospective generation from coordinates;
- prediction of confusions between neighboring coordinates;
- cross-domain transfer of learned coordinate distinctions;
- cross-linguistic or cross-cultural replication;
- behavioral signatures corresponding to topology or persistence;
- evidence that coordinate-generated forms explain variance not captured by existing semantic or task categories.

## 30.2 Formal progress

Progress would include:

- precise definitions of admissible regions;
- a metric or topology over coordinates;
- formal treatment of constraint width;
- transition systems for historical formation;
- explicit ambiguity models;
- compositional rules for stacking;
- proofs of nonredundancy or minimality where possible;
- formal conditions for out-of-atlas cases.

## 30.3 Comparative-theory progress

The framework should be compared against simpler alternatives:

- a single-axis taxonomy;
- purely semantic clustering;
- event-structure classifications;
- Gestalt closure categories;
- generic problem-solving stages;
- systems-theoretic state descriptions.

A meaningful success would show that the three-axis grammar predicts or compresses data better than these rivals.

## 30.4 Computational progress

An executable model should take inputs, infer or represent opening pressure, select coordinate constraints, and predict completed structure.

A more ambitious model would learn the three dimensions from data without being hard-coded.

## 30.5 What would count as revision or falsification?

The theory should be revised if:

- one dimension repeatedly collapses into another;
- large classes of completions cannot be represented without ad hoc extension;
- some of the 54 positions fail to yield coherent generated forms under independent analysis;
- reliable new forms fall outside the state-space;
- constraint-strength patterns fail to replicate;
- canonical classifications depend primarily on semantic content rather than coordinates;
- or prospective generation does not outperform arbitrary plausible descriptions.

A universal-exhaustiveness claim would be falsified by one well-supported irreducible form outside the grammar. Since universality is not currently claimed, such a case would presently motivate expansion rather than refutation of the entire framework.

# 32. Research Questions the Academic Landscape Imposes

The disciplinary analysis produces a concrete research agenda.

## 32.1 Ontological status

Are completed forms properties of experienced situations, representations, tasks, interpretations, or relational episodes between agents and situations?

The theory currently leaves this open.

## 32.2 Subject dependence

Can a coordinate be attributed without reference to an actual or possible interpreter?

If not, the theory is more deeply cognitive or phenomenological than its present formalism admits.

## 32.3 Primitive independence

Can experimental manipulation vary Transformation Pattern while holding Topology and Persistence constant, and likewise for the other axes?

This is the decisive empirical test of the compositional architecture.

## 32.4 Psychological reality

Do humans spontaneously discriminate the axes?

Do reaction times, confidence, memory, or neural measures reflect coordinate distance?

## 32.5 Linguistic realization

Do languages encode recurrent completion structures through systematic constructional or aspectual means?

Are some coordinates easier to lexicalize or grammaticalize than others?

## 32.6 Cross-cultural stability

Are the generated forms invariant while canonical crystallizations vary?

This would strongly support the structural/semantic distinction.

## 32.7 Constraint width

Can Near-determinate, Tightly constrained, and Open realization be operationalized quantitatively—for example through inter-generator convergence, semantic dispersion, or classification entropy?

## 32.8 Historical formation

Do Opening, governing transformation, Landing, and persistent result predict processing boundaries or retrospective reanalysis?

## 32.9 Computational learnability

Can a model recover the three axes from heterogeneous examples without direct supervision?

## 32.10 Exhaustiveness

What would a genuine out-of-atlas completion look like?

The theory should actively search for such cases rather than treating them as noise.

# 34. Twelve Direct Answers

A **functional-phenomenological constraint grammar of completion structure**: a theory that decomposes recurrent completed intelligibility into Transformation Pattern, Completion Topology, and Persistence Mode and composes those constraints into coordinate-generated event-syntaxes.

**The structural form by which a determinate opening pressure is answered sufficiently for a situation to become available for uptake.**

Primarily at a **structural-functional and phenomenological level**, above algorithmic/neural mechanism and below metaphysical or universal claims.

**Theoretical cognitive science, especially philosophy of cognitive science.**

## 5. What are its nearest neighboring disciplines?

**Phenomenology, cognitive psychology, cognitive linguistics, philosophy of language and mind, semiotics, formal semantics, and systems theory.**

## 6. Which disciplines are primarily testing or application domains?

**Linguistics, cognitive psychology, computational cognitive modeling, AI, education, social science, knowledge representation, and complexity science.**

## 7. Is it interdisciplinary, transdisciplinary, pre-disciplinary, or a candidate independent field?

It is currently best described as a **cross-disciplinary research programme within cognitive science**, not an independent field. It is a candidate subfield only if empirical and formal validation accumulates.

## 8. Does it add a new level of analysis to an existing discipline?

Potentially yes. Its strongest disciplinary contribution is the proposal of **completion structure** as a level distinct from linguistic form, semantic content, cognitive mechanism, and epistemic evaluation.

## 9. Does it redefine anything?

It **reframes completion** as sufficiency relative to opening pressure and introduces a technical structural sense of intelligibility. It does not presently redefine linguistics, cognition, phenomenology, psychology, meaning, or academia.

## 10. Does it justify a new subfield or research programme?

It justifies a **research programme now**. A subfield would become warranted if independent studies establish robust cross-domain phenomena that are not adequately captured by existing levels.

## 11. What must happen before stronger disciplinary claims become warranted?

Independent replication, operationalization of primitives, cross-domain and cross-cultural tests, predictive behavioral findings, formalization of admissible regions and transitions, and comparison with rival models.

## 12. If an academic paper had to be submitted tomorrow, which disciplinary audience should receive it first?

**Philosophy of cognitive science / theoretical cognitive science**, because the present contribution is strongest as a proposed object and level of analysis rather than as a validated mechanism.

# 35. Final Disciplinary Determination

The strongest available placement is not that the Constraint Grammar belongs nowhere. Nor is it that it already establishes a new discipline.

Its primary object is coherent enough to locate: **completion structure**—the recurring organization by which structured incompletion becomes sufficient for determinate uptake.

Its explanatory level is also identifiable: **functional-phenomenological**, describing the form of achieved organization rather than the algorithm or substrate that produces it.

No narrow discipline provides a complete home.

Phenomenology closely matches the descriptive level but is narrower in its first-person and methodological commitments and does not by itself explain the finite generative architecture.

Linguistics offers rich evidence, compositional analogies, and possible realizations, but making it the home would confuse one privileged manifestation with the theory’s object.

Cognitive psychology provides the strongest empirical machinery, but the present framework does not specify a psychological mechanism.

Formal semantics and logic provide formal resources without sharing the primary object.

Systems and complexity theories overlap in constraint, organization, and emergence but focus on systems rather than intelligible completion.

AI and computational modeling can implement or test the framework but do not define its current identity.

Cognitive science is uniquely capable of containing all of these relations without forcing premature reduction. Philosophy of cognitive science is the most appropriate immediate subdisciplinary location because the theory is presently an articulated structural hypothesis about a level of cognitive organization.

The calibrated conclusion is therefore:

> **The Constraint Grammar of Completed Intelligibility is best understood as a functional-phenomenological theory of completion structure situated within theoretical cognitive science, with philosophy of cognitive science as its immediate intellectual home and phenomenology as its closest descriptive neighbor. Its distinctive proposal is that completed intelligibility constitutes an analyzable level of organization generated by the conjunction of transformation, completion topology, and persistence. Linguistics, psychology, semiotics, systems theory, formal modeling, and AI provide major testing, comparison, or implementation domains, but none currently contains the object without reduction.**

### Current disciplinary identity

**A theoretical cognitive-science research programme in the philosophy of cognition, proposing completion structure as a functional-phenomenological level of analysis.**

### Strongest alternative identity

**A generative or formal phenomenology of completed intelligibility.**

### Why the alternative is insufficient

Because the theory is not restricted to first-person conscious experience and makes a finite compositional, cross-domain, prospectively generative claim that requires empirical and formal methods beyond phenomenological description.

### Candidate future identity if validated

**A subfield of cognitive science devoted to completion structure**, potentially organized as Completion Structure Research, integrating phenomenological description, cognitive experimentation, linguistic realization, and computational modeling.

### What it does not currently claim to be

It is not a universal grammar beneath all language.

It is not a proven cognitive architecture.

It is not a neural theory.

It is not a mathematical theory of meaning.

It is not an ontology of all possible forms of reality.

It is not an exhaustive metaphysics of intelligibility.

It is not a replacement for linguistics, psychology, phenomenology, semiotics, logic, or cognitive science.

It does not redefine academia.

Its strongest present claim is both narrower and more substantial: **there is a candidate structural level of completed intelligibility whose internally coherent organization is captured by a three-axis constraint grammar, and that level is sufficiently distinct from content, linguistic form, mechanism, and epistemic evaluation to warrant direct theoretical and empirical study.**
