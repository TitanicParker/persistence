# Chapter 17 — Take It Outside

*Pixels of Clarity*

[← Chapter 16](/persistence/pixels-of-clarity/chapter-16/) · **Chapter 17 of 18** · *No Prepared Examples*

---

The next morning there were no sentence strips.

No piles.

No Goldmine.

No cards marked **TEST IT**.

Even the fifty-four-box board had been covered with a sheet.

Leo looked around.

"What are we sorting?"

Mr Vale opened the classroom door.

"Nothing I've chosen for you."

"Then what are we doing?"

"Going outside."

---

## The rule

They were given notebooks and one instruction.

Find three things.

> **ONE:** something you think is ready for a coordinate.
>
> **TWO:** something where a neighbouring coordinate is genuinely tempting.
>
> **THREE:** something you should *not* confidently classify.

"Can we use the names?" asked Sara.

"If they help."

"Can we use the map?"

"Not yet."

"Can we ask you?"

"You can ask me anything. I may ask you why."

That was all.

They left the room.

---

## The gate

The first thing Leo found was almost too perfect.

The caretaker was working on the side gate.

It had been scraping against the ground all week.

He tightened one hinge, loosened another, lifted the gate slightly, and swung it.

The gate moved cleanly.

"There," he said.

Leo wrote immediately.

> **The gate works properly again.**

Then he stopped.

"That's suspicious."

"What is?" asked Maya.

"It's exactly the kind of thing Mr Vale would have put on a strip."

"Except he didn't."

They watched the caretaker swing the gate twice more.

"What kind of change?" Maya asked.

"Repair."

"What made it enough?"

They argued.

Was the focal point the corrected hinge alignment?

Was sufficiency distributed across several adjustments?

They had witnessed a real repair, but reality had not arrived with the second dial labelled.

Leo grinned.

"Outside is harder."

---

## The bell

Priya's first example came from the school bell.

It rang.

Children who had been scattered around the playground began moving toward their classrooms.

She wrote:

> **The bell sent everyone back inside.**

"Efficacy," said Jonah.

"Probably," said Priya.

"Why probably?"

"Because I'm describing what it did."

"So?"

"So I might be making the Efficacy obvious by the way I phrase it."

She crossed out the sentence.

Then wrote only:

> **The bell rang. The playground emptied.**

Now they had to decide what completed intelligibility they were actually examining rather than classify a sentence engineered to advertise an answer.

The world did not speak in glossary definitions.

---

## A tempting neighbour

Maya found hers in the lunch queue.

Two pupils were arguing about who had arrived first.

A dinner supervisor listened to both, asked another child what she had seen, and finally pointed.

"Ella was here first. Sam goes behind her."

The argument stopped.

Maya wrote:

> **The question of who was first was decided.**

Settlement seemed obvious.

But she wanted a neighbour.

"Could it be Differentiation?" she asked.

Priya considered it.

"Because Ella and Sam have been distinguished?"

"Yes."

"But what is the completion doing?"

They replayed the scene.

The important change was not merely that two positions had been distinguished.

A live dispute had ceased to govern the queue because an answer now fixed where the two pupils stood.

Settlement was stronger.

But Differentiation had been a real temptation.

Maya wrote beneath it:

> **A neighbour is useful when you can explain why you nearly chose it.**

---

## The thing Jonah refused to classify

Jonah came back from the football pitch with almost nothing written down.

"Couldn't find one?" asked Leo.

"Found loads. This one is annoying."

He had heard two children talking beside the goal.

> **We'll probably change teams after break.**

Leo began immediately.

"Opening—"

"No," said Jonah.

"You don't even know what I was going to say."

"You're trying to finish it for them."

The possibility was live.

The change had not occurred.

The arrangement had not been made sufficient.

Its persistence was not yet an achieved result.

Jonah had found his third case.

> **NO COMPLETION YET.**

And for once, refusing the coordinate felt like success.

---

## Sara finds a different kind of refusal

Sara's difficult case was not unfinished.

It was overheard near the library.

> **That's clear now.**

Nothing else.

She knew from Chapter 14 that the phrase could belong to very different structural stories.

Maybe a disagreement had been settled.

Maybe a distinction had been clarified.

Maybe a practical arrangement had been fixed.

The words alone did not tell her enough.

She wrote:

> **NEED CONTEXT.**

Then underneath:

> **Not knowing because evidence is thin is different from not knowing because nothing is complete.**

The categories of uncertainty had survived outside too.

---

## Nobody was waiting to be classified

When they returned to class, the board filled with observations:

- a jammed drawer made usable again;
- two teams formed from one group;
- a disagreement over a missing pencil resolved;
- a route through a crowded corridor opened;
- a timetable change announced and then followed;
- paint colours mixed until a particular shade was reached;
- a loose shoelace tied;
- an unfinished plan for after school;
- a sentence overheard without enough context;
- a claim about who had taken a ball that nobody had yet checked.

Some cases were clean.

Some were messy.

Some contained several possible completed intelligibilities depending on what exactly the observer chose to examine.

That mattered.

The world had not been waiting for fifty-four labels.

The children had to decide what they were attending to and justify why.

---

## The map comes back

Only then did Mr Vale pull the sheet from the board.

The fifty-four boxes were exactly where they had always been.

But they looked different.

Less like destinations.

More like a set of contrasts.

The children placed their strongest cases.

For each one, the class could challenge:

> **Why that transformation?**
>
> **Why that completion topology?**
>
> **Why that persistence mode?**
>
> **What is the nearest tempting neighbour?**
>
> **What would have to change for you to move one dial?**

Nobody was allowed to defend a classification by saying only:

> **It sounds like one of those.**

---

## Then Mr Vale reversed the game

When the board was full, Mr Vale erased every example.

He left only the coordinates.

"Now the other direction."

"What other direction?" asked Leo.

Mr Vale pointed to one box.

> **Repair × Distributed × Efficacy**

"Make it speak."

Chapter 13 again.

But this time there was no Goldmine to choose from.

The children had to generate ordinary language themselves.

Maya tried:

> **After we adjusted all the parts, the machine started working properly again.**

"Too easy," said Leo.

"Why?"

"You practically said Distributed and Efficacy out loud."

He was right.

They revised it.

> **Once we'd worked across the whole setup, it could do its job again.**

Better.

Still arguable.

And argument was the point.

---

## Make the neighbour too

Priya added a harder rule.

"If we make one for a box, we should make one for the neighbour that would fool us."

They kept Repair and Distributed fixed and moved the third dial.

### Standing

> **After all the adjustments, the setup was back in its proper condition.**

### Bearing

> **After all the adjustments, the parts sat correctly in relation to one another.**

### Efficacy

> **After all the adjustments, the setup could do its job again.**

Three utterances.

One dial moved.

Now the differences were not merely something they could recognize.

They could **produce** the contrast.

---

## Recognition is only half of fluency

Mr Vale wrote two arrows.

> **ordinary case → coordinate**
>
> **coordinate → ordinary case**

"Which one have we done more?" he asked.

"The first," said Sara.

"The Goldmine did the second for us," said Jonah.

"Until now," said Maya.

They spent the afternoon moving both ways.

A coordinate was called out.

Someone invented a natural utterance.

Another person produced its nearest neighbour.

A third person tried to break both with a different interpretation.

Then somebody brought in an observed case from outside and the direction reversed again.

They were no longer only solving the teacher's examples.

They were making examples capable of testing one another.

---

## The ten-year-old test returns

Leo produced this:

> **The distributed relational configuration persists through efficacious integration.**

Everyone stared at him.

"What?" said Sara.

"I can point to the box."

"That's not the game."

"Why not?"

Maya reminded him of Chapter 13.

> **Could a bright ten-year-old understand when somebody might actually say this?**

Leo looked at his sentence.

"No human being would say that."

"You just did."

"I withdraw the accusation against humanity."

They tried again.

The formal vocabulary was useful for compression between people who already understood the distinctions.

It was not evidence that the distinctions had been understood.

If anything, ordinary language was now the harder test.

---

## Homework without a worksheet

At the end of the day, Mr Vale gave them homework.

There was no sheet.

"Bring me one thing tomorrow."

"One what?" asked Leo.

"You decide."

"A sentence?"

"If you like."

"An event?"

"If you like."

"A coordinate?"

"If you like."

"That's terrible homework."

"Why?"

"How do we know when we're done?"

Mr Vale smiled.

Nobody else did.

They had heard that question before.

---

## The next morning

Leo brought a bus ticket.

Priya brought a note about an argument between her brother and sister.

Jonah brought a photograph of a shelf his father had put up crooked and then corrected.

Sara brought a sentence her mother had said while cooking.

Maya brought nothing physical.

"What did you bring?" asked Leo.

"A question."

"Of course you did."

She wrote it on the board.

> **If we can find these structures without the teacher, and make examples without the Goldmine, what exactly have we learned?**

Nobody answered immediately.

The fifty-four boxes were still on the wall.

The three questions were still useful.

The fifteen names were still available.

But the children were beginning to suspect that none of those things was the final achievement.

---

## Mr Vale reaches under his desk

"I brought something too," he said.

He reached under the desk.

There was a soft thump.

Then another.

He lifted an old canvas bag onto the table.

Leo recognized it first.

"No."

Sara laughed.

Maya was already standing.

The bag was creased now.

One handle had a strip of tape around it.

But it was the same bag.

The one from the beginning.

Mr Vale put his hand on the zip.

"Tomorrow," he said.

"That's cruel," said Leo.

"Probably."

"What's in it?"

Mr Vale looked at the fifty-four-box board.

Then at the class.

"Let's find out whether you still need me to tell you what to do with it."
