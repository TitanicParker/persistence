# Chapter 6 — Three Ways a Change Becomes Complete

*Pixels of Clarity*

[← Chapter 5](/persistence/pixels-of-clarity/chapter-05/) · **Chapter 6 of 18** · *Completion Gets Its Shape*

---

The three temporary labels were still on the table.

> **ENOUGH HERE**
>
> **ENOUGH ACROSS IT**
>
> **NOT IN CHARGE ANYMORE**

Nobody thought those were the final names.

But everybody knew what they were pointing at.

Yesterday, the children had held the first question still.

Everything had been **Repair**.

So the question was no longer:

> **What kind of change happened?**

It was:

> **What made that change count as complete?**

The teacher wrote three new words on the board.

> **Focal**
>
> **Distributed**
>
> **Release**

A child pointed at the first one.

“Which is which?”

The teacher did not answer.

Another child moved **Focal** beneath **ENOUGH HERE**.

“Probably that one.”

Then **Distributed** beneath **ENOUGH ACROSS IT**.

“And that one.”

Which left **Release**.

Someone looked at **NOT IN CHARGE ANYMORE**.

“That one actually makes sense.”

The teacher nodded.

“Now make sure the names survive the examples.”

---

## Focal

The first formal word was:

> **Focal**

The class returned to the sentence:

> **That part is fixed now.**

What made the Repair complete?

Not that every part had been repaired.

Not that an old fault had lost its influence everywhere.

The completion gathered around a particular locus.

The teacher gave the sharper formulation:

> **Focal: the transformation becomes complete because completion gathers around one privileged locus.**

“Privileged?”

“Important in the structure of this completion.”

“So Focal means one thing?”

“No.”

That was the first trap.

The teacher put down:

> **At this crossing, the two routes finally separate clearly.**

There might be hundreds of roads on the map.

There might be several signs.

There might be dozens of people using the route.

Focal did not mean the whole situation contained only one thing.

It meant that **why the transformation counts as complete gathers around one decisive locus**.

The class shortened that to:

> **Focal: this completion turns on a particular point.**

Then they tested it outside Repair.

> **At this mark, we can finally tell the two measurements apart.**

Differentiation — and the completion gathers around the decisive mark.

> **This doorway gives us the way through.**

Opening — and the completion gathers around the relevant access point.

The first new word seemed to hold.

---

## Distributed

The second word was:

> **Distributed**

The class already knew the feel of it.

> **The faults across the whole system have been put right.**

No single repaired point was enough by itself.

The repair counted as complete only across a relevant plurality, span, sequence, range, or system.

The formal version was:

> **Distributed: the transformation becomes complete only across a constitutive plurality, span, sequence, range, reciprocal field, set of cases, or system. No single locus is sufficient by itself.**

Someone stared at the sentence.

“Can we keep our version too?”

Of course.

> **Distributed: it is only enough across the spread that matters.**

Then came the obvious mistake.

“So Distributed means lots of things.”

“No.”

The teacher placed down two sentences:

> **Three tools are lying on the table.**

and

> **The whole checklist is now complete across all three stages.**

The first sentence has several things in it.

That does not make its completion Distributed.

The second sentence makes its sufficiency depend on the relevant spread.

The distinction was not **one thing versus many things**.

It was:

> **Does completion depend on one privileged locus, or only on the relevant plurality as a field?**

The children tried examples from other Transformation families.

> **Across all the samples, the difference is now clear.**

Differentiation × Distributed.

> **Every stage of the route is now open.**

Opening × Distributed.

> **The parts across the system now form one organized whole.**

Integration × Distributed.

The second word held too.

---

## Release

Then came the strange one.

> **Release**

The temporary label was:

> **NOT IN CHARGE ANYMORE**

The teacher put back the Repair sentence:

> **The old fault no longer controls what happens.**

“What made the Repair complete?”

“The old problem is not deciding the result anymore.”

“Does Release mean the old problem vanished?”

“No.”

“Does it mean it was removed?”

“Not necessarily.”

“Does it mean nothing is there?”

“No.”

This was the important distinction.

Release did not mean simple absence.

It did not mean movement away.

It did not mean separation.

It did not mean negation.

It meant **de-governance**.

Something that had been determining how the situation went had lost that determining force.

The formal version was:

> **Release: the transformation becomes complete because something that previously governed the situation loses its determining force.**

The class kept the shorter version beneath it:

> **Release: what used to be in charge of how this went is not in charge anymore.**

That wording was not perfect.

But it made the right question easier to ask.

---

## Release is not “gone”

The teacher put down four sentences.

> **The box has been removed from the room.**
>
> **The rule no longer prevents us from continuing.**
>
> **The old mistake no longer affects the calculation.**
>
> **The rope has been taken away.**

“Which ones clearly show Release?”

The middle two.

The first and last might describe removal.

But removal alone does not tell us that something which governed the relevant completion has lost its determining force.

The children wrote a warning card:

> **Gone is not enough.**

Then another:

> **Ask what stopped governing.**

That was a better Release test.

---

## The same three shapes across different changes

Now the teacher stopped using Repair.

The children had to see whether Focal, Distributed, and Release were truly a new dimension rather than three special kinds of Repair.

### Differentiation

> **At this mark, the difference becomes clear.**

The completion gathers around a particular locus.

**Focal.**

> **Across the whole set, the differences are now clear.**

No one point is sufficient.

**Distributed.**

> **The old blur no longer prevents us from telling them apart.**

What had governed the indistinction has lost its force.

**Release.**

### Opening

> **This gate gives us the way through.**

**Focal.**

> **The route is open all the way across.**

**Distributed.**

> **The rule no longer blocks us from continuing.**

**Release.**

### Settlement

> **This decision settles the issue.**

**Focal.**

> **All the outstanding points have now been settled.**

**Distributed.**

> **The old claim no longer demands an answer from us.**

**Release.**

The children noticed something important.

Focal, Distributed, and Release did not tell them **what kind of change happened**.

They told them something different:

> **what shape made that change sufficient.**

That was why the first question and the second question could not replace one another.

---

## Two questions now

The board now had two questions.

### Question 1

> **What kind of change happened?**

Six answers:

- Differentiation
- Opening
- Relating
- Repair
- Integration
- Settlement

### Question 2

> **What made that change count as complete?**

Three answers:

- Focal
- Distributed
- Release

Someone did the multiplication.

“Six times three is eighteen.”

The teacher looked at the board.

“Yes.”

“So there are eighteen boxes?”

“Not yet.”

That answer was annoying.

Which usually meant there was another problem hiding somewhere.

---

## A two-question sort

The class tried fresh sentences.

> **The error at this one point has been corrected.**

“What kind of change?”

“Repair.”

“What made it complete?”

“Focal.”

So:

> **Repair × Focal**

Then:

> **Every broken section across the fence has been restored.**

**Repair × Distributed.**

Then:

> **The old defect no longer makes the machine fail.**

**Repair × Release.**

Then:

> **This sign is the point where the two routes become distinguishable.**

**Differentiation × Focal.**

Then:

> **The whole range of options is now available.**

**Opening × Distributed.**

Then:

> **The old restriction no longer prevents us from entering.**

**Opening × Release.**

The children could now narrow a sentence much further.

First choose one of six.

Then one of three.

That left a much smaller region of the system.

And yet something still did not line up.

---

## The next disagreement

One group was sorting Repair × Focal cards.

They had these three:

> **That part is fixed now.**
>
> **That part is right because it now lines up with the guide.**
>
> **Fixing that part makes the machine work again.**

Everyone agreed on the first two answers.

**Repair.**

**Focal.**

But the cards still did not feel identical.

One child pointed to the first.

“This one just gives us the fixed result.”

Then the second.

“This one is only right because of how it fits with something else.”

Then the third.

“And this one matters because now the machine can work.”

Nobody had a name for that difference.

But the difference was there.

The teacher did not write another word on the board.

A child did.

> **What is left when the change is done?**

Another child changed it slightly:

> **How does the finished result stay available?**

The class looked at the three Repair × Focal cards again.

They had found the next question themselves.

>

> **Do they still leave the same kind of result?**

If the answer is no, keep the pair.

You have found the problem for the next chapter.

---

## Next: What Is Left When It Is Done?

We now have two questions.

They tell us:

- what kind of change happened;
- what made that change sufficient.

But two sentences can share both answers and still leave different kinds of completed result.

So the next question is not about the change itself.

And it is not about what made the change enough.

It is about what remains available once the completion has been achieved.
