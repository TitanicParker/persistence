# Chapter 3 — The First Question

*Pixels of Clarity*

[← Chapter 2](/persistence/pixels-of-clarity/chapter-02/) · **Chapter 3 of 18** · *The First Six Families*

---

The teacher did something different the next day.

She did not bring out the whole bag.

She brought six envelopes.

Each envelope had only a handful of sentence strips inside it.

No labels.

No numbers.

No instructions except one question on the board:

> **What kind of change happened?**

Underneath it, in smaller writing:

> **What kind of unresolvedness changed?**

Someone frowned at the second version.

“What does *unresolvedness* mean?”

The teacher pointed to the first question.

“For now, use that one.”

So they did.

---

## One question, many answers

The first envelope contained these sentences:

> **Now we can tell them apart.**
>
> **These two are different after all.**
>
> **I know which one is mine now.**
>
> **The mark shows which side is which.**

The children read them aloud.

“What changed?” asked the teacher.

“We can tell the difference now.”

“Between what?”

“It doesn’t matter exactly. The point is that before they were mixed up somehow.”

The teacher wrote:

> **A difference starts to matter.**

Not as an answer.

As a temporary label.

The second envelope contained:

> **There is a way through now.**
>
> **The path is open again.**
>
> **We can finally continue.**
>
> **That option is available now.**

“What changed?”

“Something that was blocked isn’t blocked anymore.”

“Always blocked?”

“No. Maybe unavailable.”

“Or impossible before.”

The teacher wrote:

> **Something becomes available.**

The children were already noticing that the first envelope and the second were not merely about different topics.

They were doing different structural moves.

---

## The third envelope

The next set was harder.

> **This tells us how the two things compare.**
>
> **One depends on the other.**
>
> **Their positions only make sense in relation to each other.**
>
> **What happens here changes what happens there.**

“What changed?”

“They got connected.”

“Were they necessarily disconnected before?”

Nobody answered.

The teacher waited.

A child tried again.

“Maybe the connection became important.”

Another said:

“The relationship became part of what the situation is.”

The teacher wrote:

> **How things bear on each other becomes important.**

Someone pointed at the board.

“That one is much longer.”

“It is,” said the teacher.

“Can we shorten it?”

“Later.”

That answer became important too.

The class was not trying to invent perfect names yet.

They were trying to see the family clearly enough that a good name might eventually become possible.

---

## A very easy envelope

The fourth envelope looked almost too simple.

> **The broken wheel turns properly again.**
>
> **That part is fixed now.**
>
> **The mistake has been corrected.**
>
> **The system is back in order.**

“What changed?”

“Something was wrong and now it’s right.”

“Right in what sense?”

“Working the way it should.”

The teacher wrote:

> **A fault stops governing the result.**

A few children objected.

“That is not how we said it.”

“No,” said the teacher. “Your version was easier. Mine is more exact.”

She added their version underneath:

> **Something was wrong and now it has been put right.**

Two sentences for the same family.

One easier.

One sharper.

The class kept both.

---

## Parts becoming one thing

The fifth envelope contained:

> **The pieces now work as one.**
>
> **Everyone has a part to play.**
>
> **The ingredients now make one complete dish.**
>
> **The separate jobs fit into one plan.**

“What kind of change happened?”

“Things joined together.”

“Is joining enough?”

The teacher put two paper clips side by side.

“They are together.”

Nobody thought that was enough.

She clipped them.

“Now they’re joined.”

Still not enough.

A child pointed to the sentences.

“In these, the parts have jobs.”

“And they make a whole.”

“And the whole can do something because of how the parts fit.”

The teacher wrote:

> **Separate parts gain places in a whole.**

That pile had a stronger shape now.

---

## The last envelope

The sixth envelope contained:

> **That settles it.**
>
> **We have agreed what happens next.**
>
> **There is nothing more to decide.**
>
> **We can leave the matter there.**

“What changed?”

“Something finished.”

The teacher pointed back to the repair pile.

“That was finished too.”

“Something was decided.”

She pointed to a sentence from the first envelope.

“Knowing which one is which can feel decided.”

The class looked again.

One child said:

“There was still something that had to be answered, and now there isn’t.”

The teacher stopped writing for a moment.

Then she put that sentence on the board exactly as it had been said:

> **There was still something that had to be answered, and now there isn’t.**

Nobody tried to shorten it.

Not yet.

---

## Six piles, no names

By now the class had six working piles.

They had temporary descriptions, but no official names.

The board looked roughly like this:

1. **A difference starts to matter.**
2. **Something becomes available.**
3. **How things bear on each other becomes important.**
4. **Something was wrong and now it has been put right.**
5. **Separate parts gain places in a whole.**
6. **Something that still needed an answer no longer does.**

Someone counted them.

“Six.”

“Yes.”

“Are there only six?”

The teacher shrugged.

“Test them.”

That was the rule now.

A number was not a discovery until the piles could survive new cards.

---

## The test cards

The teacher brought out twelve new strips.

> **Now we know which footprint belongs to which animal.**
>
> **The password lets us in.**
>
> **The score tells us who is ahead.**
>
> **The leak has been sealed.**
>
> **The choir finally sings as one group.**
>
> **The argument is over.**
>
> **The new rule gives everyone a chance to join.**
>
> **This number depends on the size of that one.**
>
> **The parts now support each other.**
>
> **We can see where the two routes separate.**
>
> **Nothing more is owed between us.**
>
> **The machine is working properly again.**

“Do not make new piles unless you need them,” said the teacher.

“Why?”

“Because now we are testing a guess.”

The guess was that the six piles had found six deep kinds of change.

If a new sentence did not fit any of them, perhaps the guess was wrong.

If one sentence seemed to fit two, perhaps the piles were still too blurry.

If the same pile kept accepting very different topics, that was good evidence that the class was seeing something structural.

---

## A sentence can fit badly for a good reason

One card caused trouble:

> **The password lets us in.**

Some children put it with:

> **Something becomes available.**

Others put it with:

> **A difference starts to matter.**

“Because the password tells the system who is allowed in,” one child argued.

“But the sentence doesn’t say that,” another replied. “It says what becomes possible.”

The teacher drew a circle around the card.

“This is useful.”

Nobody groaned this time.

By now they knew what a troublesome card meant.

It meant they had to distinguish:

- what the sentence **contains**;
- what the sentence **mainly completes**.

The password may work by distinguishing one input from another.

But in the sentence **“The password lets us in,”** the important completed change is that access has become available.

The class moved it to the second pile.

Then one child said:

“So we’re not asking what had to happen in the background.”

“Right.”

“We’re asking what kind of change the sentence is giving us as complete.”

The teacher nodded.

That was a much better question.

---

## The first structural habit

The class now had a procedure.

When a new strip appeared, they did not ask first:

- What words are in it?
- What is it about?
- Which other sentence sounds similar?

They asked:

> **What kind of change happened?**

And if that was too vague:

> **What was unresolved before, and what is different now?**

That second question was harder.

But it helped.

For example:

> **We can see where the two routes separate.**

Before: the routes were not clearly distinguished at the relevant point.

After: the difference between them became usable.

Or:

> **The choir finally sings as one group.**

Before: the singers were separate contributions without the achieved organization of one functioning whole.

After: their parts had become organized together.

Or:

> **The argument is over.**

Before: something still demanded further response, decision, or continuation.

After: that demand had been discharged.

The class did not yet know the formal names.

But they were beginning to see the six structural families without them.

---

## Your turn: name the piles your way

Here are twelve sentences.

Try to place each into one of the six unnamed families.

1. **The two shades are clearly different now.**
2. **We can finally enter the room.**
3. **This result changes when that value changes.**
4. **The damaged hinge works properly again.**
5. **The five jobs now fit into one plan.**
6. **We have decided who goes first.**
7. **Now I can tell which voice is which.**
8. **There is room for one more person now.**
9. **The two sides now depend on each other.**
10. **The error has been corrected.**
11. **The players now work as one team.**
12. **That question has been answered.**

Do not use the official names. You do not know them yet.

Make your own six pile names.

Then check each name against all the sentences in its pile.

A good pile name should not merely describe one example.

It should say what kind of change keeps happening across the whole family.

---

## A better test than “Does it fit?”

By the end of the lesson, the children had learned not to be satisfied with:

> **Does this sentence fit here?**

A better question was:

> **Why does it fit here better than in the other five piles?**

That small change mattered.

To understand a family, you had to understand its boundaries.

A repaired thing may become usable again, but repair is not the same as opening.

Two things may become organized together, but integration is not merely relating.

A settled matter may involve a distinction, but settlement is not differentiation.

The piles were beginning to sharpen one another.

They were not six isolated boxes.

They were six answers to one question.

---

## The names are coming

At the end of the day, the teacher wrote the six temporary descriptions on six large cards and pinned them to the wall.

No formal names yet.

A child looked at them and said:

“These are going to have proper names, aren’t they?”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you just tell us the names first?”

The teacher pointed at the piles on the floor.

“Because now the names will have something to attach to.”

The children already knew what each family felt like.

Tomorrow they would learn the words.

And, more importantly, they would find out whether those six words were exact enough to survive the difficult cards.
