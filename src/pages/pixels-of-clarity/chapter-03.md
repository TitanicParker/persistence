# Chapter 3 — The First Question

*Pixels of Clarity*

[← Chapter 2](/persistence/pixels-of-clarity/chapter-02/) · **Chapter 3 of 18** · *The First Six Families*

---

The teacher did something different the next day.

She did not bring out the whole bag.

She brought six envelopes.

Each envelope had only a handful of sentence strips inside it.

No labels. No numbers. One question:

> **What kind of change happened?**

Underneath it, in smaller writing:

> **What was unresolved before, and what is different now?**

That was all they were allowed to ask for now.

---

## One question, many answers

The first envelope said:

> **Now we can tell them apart.**
>
> **These two are different after all.**
>
> **I know which one is mine now.**
>
> **The mark shows which side is which.**

“We can tell the difference now.”

A child wrote a temporary label:

> **A difference starts to matter.**

The second envelope said:

> **There is a way through now.**
>
> **The path is open again.**
>
> **We can finally continue.**
>
> **That option is available now.**

“Something becomes possible or available.”

Another child wrote:

> **Something becomes available.**

Already the first two piles were different for a reason that had nothing to do with topic.

---

## The third envelope

> **This tells us how the two things compare.**
>
> **One depends on the other.**
>
> **Their positions make sense through each other.**
>
> **What happens here changes what happens there.**

“They got connected.”

“Were they necessarily disconnected before?” someone asked.

That stopped the group.

“Maybe the important change is that how they stand with each other is now part of the answer.”

They tried:

> **How things stand with each other becomes important.**

It was not a perfect name. It did not need to be.

The job was to see the family first.

---

## The fourth envelope

> **The broken wheel turns properly again.**
>
> **That part is fixed now.**
>
> **The mistake has been corrected.**
>
> **The system is back in order.**

“Something was wrong and now it’s right.”

“Right in what sense?” another child asked.

“Working the way it should.”

They wrote:

> **Something failing has been put right enough.**

The teacher did not improve it.

Not yet.

The children had found the distinction they needed.

---

## Parts becoming one thing

The fifth envelope said:

> **The pieces now work as one.**
>
> **Everyone has a part to play.**
>
> **The ingredients now make one complete dish.**
>
> **The separate jobs fit into one plan.**

“Things joined together.”

“Is joining enough?”

Two paper clips lying side by side were together. Two clipped together were joined. Neither example captured the sentences.

“In these, the parts have jobs.”

“And the jobs belong in one whole.”

They wrote:

> **Separate parts gain places in a whole.**

---

## The last envelope

> **That settles it.**
>
> **We have agreed what happens next.**
>
> **There is nothing more to decide.**
>
> **We can leave the matter there.**

“Something finished.”

“But the repaired wheel finished too.”

“Something was decided.”

“But not every one of these is a decision.”

Then someone said:

> **There was still something that needed an answer, and now there isn’t.**

That stayed on the board exactly as it was.

---

## Six piles, no names

The board now held six working descriptions:

1. **A difference starts to matter.**
2. **Something becomes available.**
3. **How things stand with each other becomes important.**
4. **Something failing has been put right enough.**
5. **Separate parts gain places in a whole.**
6. **Something that still needed an answer no longer does.**

Someone counted.

“Six.”

“Are there only six?”

The teacher shrugged.

“Test them.”

A number was not a discovery until the piles could survive new cards.

---

## The test cards

Twelve new strips appeared:

> **Now we know which footprint belongs to which animal.**
>
> **The password lets us in.**
>
> **The score tells us who is ahead.**
>
> **The leak has been sealed.**
>
> **The choir finally sings as one group.**
>
> **The argument is over.**
>
> **The new rule gives everyone a chance to join.**
>
> **This number depends on the size of that one.**
>
> **The parts now support each other.**
>
> **We can see where the two routes separate.**
>
> **Nothing more is owed between us.**
>
> **The machine is working properly again.**

“Do not make a new pile unless you need one.”

Now the children were testing a guess: perhaps these really were six recurring kinds of change.

If a sentence fit nowhere, the guess might be wrong.

If it seemed to fit two piles, the boundary might still be blurry.

If one pile accepted very different topics, that was useful evidence that the pile was structural rather than topical.

---

## A troublesome password

> **The password lets us in.**

Some children chose **Something becomes available**.

Others chose **A difference starts to matter**.

“The password distinguishes the right input from the wrong one.”

“But that isn’t the change this sentence gives us,” another child said. “It says we can get in.”

That distinction mattered.

A sentence can contain several things that make its event possible. The question here is narrower:

> **What kind of change is this sentence presenting?**

For **The password lets us in**, access becomes available.

The card went into the second pile.

Nobody needed the teacher to place it.

---

## The first structural habit

The class now had a procedure.

They did not begin with:

- What words are in it?
- What is it about?
- Which sentence sounds similar?

They began with:

> **What kind of change happened?**

And when that was too vague:

> **What was unresolved before, and what is different now?**

For example:

> **We can see where the two routes separate.**

Before: the relevant difference between the routes was not clear enough to use.

After: it is.

> **The choir finally sings as one group.**

Before: the contributions were not yet organized as one whole.

After: they are.

> **The argument is over.**

Before: the matter still called for further response.

After: it does not.

No second or third question was needed yet.

The class was learning to hold one distinction steady long enough to see it clearly.

---

## Your turn: name the piles your way

Place each sentence into one of the six unnamed families:

1. **The two shades are clearly different now.**
2. **We can finally enter the room.**
3. **This result changes when that value changes.**
4. **The damaged hinge works properly again.**
5. **The five jobs now fit into one plan.**
6. **We have decided who goes first.**
7. **Now I can tell which voice is which.**
8. **There is room for one more person now.**
9. **The two sides now depend on each other.**
10. **The error has been corrected.**
11. **The players now work as one team.**
12. **That question has been answered.**

Do not use official names. You do not know them yet.

Make your own six pile names, then test each name against every sentence in its pile.

A good name should say what kind of change keeps happening across the family.

---

## Better than “Does it fit?”

The stronger question was now:

> **Why does it fit here better than in the other five piles?**

A repaired thing may become usable, but putting something right is not the same kind of change as making access possible.

Things may depend on each other inside an organized whole, but relating is not the same kind of change as organizing parts into a whole.

A settled matter may contain a distinction, but bringing a live demand to sufficiency is not the same kind of change as making a difference determinate.

The piles sharpened one another.

They were six answers to one question.

---

## The names are coming

At the end of the day the six temporary descriptions went on the wall.

“These are going to have proper names, aren’t they?”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you tell us the names first?”

“Because now the names will have something to attach to.”

The discovery had already happened.

Next came the handles.
