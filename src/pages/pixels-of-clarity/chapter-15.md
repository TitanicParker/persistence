# Chapter 15 — Sometimes There Is No Box Yet

*Pixels of Clarity*

[← Chapter 14](/persistence/pixels-of-clarity/chapter-14/) · **Chapter 15 of 18** · *No Completion, No Coordinate*

---

The first strip on the table said:

> **Almost fixed.**

Leo reached for the map.

Maya put her hand over it.

"Don't."

"Why?"

"Because nothing's finished."

"It's Repair."

"Maybe the change is repair-like. But is there a completed intelligibility yet?"

Leo looked back at the strip.

Almost fixed.

Not fixed.

The repair was still live.

The unresolvedness had not yet been brought to sufficiency.

Priya wrote on the board:

> **NO COMPLETION, NO COORDINATE.**

That sentence changed the map more than adding another box ever could.

---

## The map is not hungry

For weeks, the children had become very good at locating things.

That created a new danger.

Once you own a map, everything starts to look like something that ought to be mapped.

Mr Vale put four strips on the table:

> **We're still deciding.**
>
> **Maybe tomorrow.**
>
> **The gate is nearly open.**
>
> **I haven't worked out which one matters yet.**

"Coordinates?" he asked.

Nobody moved.

Sara said, "They make sense."

"Yes."

"But they're not completed in the way the map is about."

Exactly.

The grammar did not claim that unfinished expressions were meaningless.

It claimed something narrower:

> **A coordinate belongs to a completed intelligibility.**

If the relevant completion has not happened, classification is premature.

---

## Meaningful is not the same as complete

They tested the difference.

> **The answer might be seven.**

Perfectly understandable.

But the determination had not settled.

> **We're choosing between two routes.**

Perfectly understandable.

But the opening or settlement had not completed.

> **The parts are starting to fit together.**

Perfectly understandable.

But Integration may still be underway.

The class wrote:

> **Intelligible expression does not automatically mean completed intelligibility.**

That distinction protected the map from becoming a universal sorting machine for every utterance.

---

## A process can have a direction without having a coordinate

Jonah objected.

"But if we know it's heading toward Repair, why can't we put it in Repair?"

"Because Transformation Pattern is only one dimension," said Maya.

"And because heading toward completion isn't the same as completion," added Priya.

They imagined a mechanic working on a bicycle.

Halfway through, you might reasonably say:

> **He's repairing it.**

That tells you something about the process.

But the three-coordinate grammar asks about a completed intelligibility:

- what transformation has been achieved;
- what made that change sufficient;
- how the achieved result remains available.

Before completion, Questions 2 and 3 may not yet have determinate answers.

The process can have a direction without having a final coordinate.

---

## The temptation to guess the ending

Mr Vale gave them a story fragment:

A class is arguing over where to hold a school fair.

One group wants the hall.

Another wants the playground.

Someone suggests the library.

The meeting pauses for lunch.

"Settlement," said Leo.

Everyone looked at him.

"I mean it will probably end in Settlement."

"Probably isn't a coordinate," said Sara.

That became another rule:

> **Do not classify the ending you expect. Classify the completion that has actually been made intelligible.**

Prediction was not completion.

---

## Some sentences report incompletion explicitly

The children began collecting phrases that signal live unresolvedness:

> **not yet**
>
> **still deciding**
>
> **almost**
>
> **trying to**
>
> **might**
>
> **hasn't been settled**
>
> **remains unclear**

But then Maya caught the next shortcut.

"Those words don't automatically mean no coordinate."

She was right.

> **It remains unclear why the alarm failed.**

Could itself be a completed determination about the state of knowledge.

> **The repair is almost finished.**

Could be a completed report about the progress of a process.

The question was not whether the sentence contained words like *still* or *almost*.

The question was:

> **What is this utterance making complete?**

Sometimes an utterance about incompletion can itself be a completed intelligibility.

That was subtle.

And important.

---

## Completion is always completion of something

They compared:

> **We are still deciding.**

As an attempt to classify the decision itself, no coordinate yet.

But as a completed report about the present state of the discussion, the utterance may itself stand as a determination.

This forced them to become more careful about the unit being classified.

The map did not attach to the whole world-situation once and for all.

It attached to the completed intelligibility under examination.

Maya wrote:

> **Ask what has been completed, not whether everything in the situation is finished.**

---

## The broken sentence test

Leo tried to defeat the rule with fragments:

> **Because the latch...**

> **When the meeting ends...**

> **If we can just...**

"No boxes," he said.

"Probably," said Priya.

"Probably?"

"A fragment can still be enough in context."

Someone pointing at a repaired wheel might say:

> **Fixed.**

One word.

Yet the context could make the completed intelligibility perfectly clear.

So grammatical completeness and structural completion were not the same thing either.

They added:

> **A full sentence can be structurally unfinished. A fragment can sometimes crystallize a completed intelligibility.**

The map cared about intelligible completion, not school grammar.

---

## Refusing a coordinate is a skill

Mr Vale put ten cards on the floor.

Some had strong coordinates.

Some needed context.

Some were genuinely ambiguous.

Some described processes still underway.

The class had to sort them into:

> **COORDINATE READY**
>
> **NEED MORE CONTEXT**
>
> **NO COMPLETION YET**

At first, Leo kept trying to rescue the third pile.

By the end, he stopped.

"It feels weird to leave something unclassified."

"Why?" asked Maya.

"Because it feels unfinished."

Priya smiled.

"Exactly."

Sometimes leaving it unclassified was the accurate result.

---

## The map has a domain

This was the first time the class used the word **domain**.

Not as in a website.

As in the range of things a tool is built to describe.

The coordinate grammar had a domain:

> **completed intelligibility**

Outside that domain were many things worth understanding:

questions,

possibilities,

attempts,

ongoing processes,

fragments,

open demands,

unresolved conflicts,

uncertain hypotheses.

The map did not become weaker by having a domain.

It became more precise.

A ruler is not bad because it does not measure temperature.

A thermometer is not bad because it does not tell you distance.

A grammar of completed intelligibility did not need to classify every meaningful thing.

---

## Your turn: coordinate or not yet?

For each, decide whether there is enough completed intelligibility to attempt a coordinate.

1. **We're still working out the final amount.**
2. **The final amount is twelve.**
3. **The gate is nearly open.**
4. **The gate is open now.**
5. **They are trying to agree on the terms.**
6. **They agreed on the terms.**
7. **Almost there.**
8. **That's enough; we're there.**

Then ask the harder question:

> **Could any of the “not yet” utterances themselves become completed intelligibilities under a different reading?**

Explain how.

---

## One old question gets a better answer

The card from Chapter 11 still said:

> **DOES EVERY COMPLETED INTELLIGIBILITY HAVE TO LAND SOMEWHERE ON THIS MAP?**

They were not ready to answer that universally.

But they could now rule out one bad version of the question.

Not every meaningful utterance needed a coordinate.

Not every event needed a coordinate.

Not every process needed a coordinate while it was unfolding.

The grammar was about completed intelligibility.

That was already a major limit.

And limits made claims testable.

---

## A new question arrives

At the end of the lesson, Sara held up a card from the **COORDINATE READY** pile.

> **The treasure is buried under the oak tree.**

"Suppose that has a perfectly clear coordinate," she said.

"Okay," said Leo.

"What if there is no treasure?"

The room went quiet.

Maya looked at the question wall.

Then she wrote:

> **CAN SOMETHING BE PERFECTLY INTELLIGIBLE AND STILL BE FALSE?**

Everyone knew the answer was probably yes.

But now they had to understand why.
