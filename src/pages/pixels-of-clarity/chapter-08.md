# Chapter 8 — Why Does This Keep Showing Up?

*Pixels of Clarity*

[← Chapter 7](/persistence/pixels-of-clarity/chapter-07/) · **Chapter 8 of 18** · *The Bend*

---

The next morning, there were no sentence strips on the tables.

That was the first thing everyone noticed.

For weeks, the strips had been everywhere. In piles. In envelopes. Under books. Once, somehow, inside Maya's lunchbox.

Today the tables were empty.

On the board were the three questions the class had written the day before:

> **What kind of change happened?**
>
> **What made that change enough?**
>
> **How does the achieved result remain available?**

Nobody had erased them.

Mr Vale was at his desk, fixing the loose arm on a pair of glasses.

He tightened a tiny screw, folded the glasses, opened them again, and put them on.

"Fixed," said Maya.

Jonah pointed at the board.

"Repair."

Priya laughed. "You can't help it now."

Jonah shrugged. "The screw was the place that had to be put right. And now the arm stays where it's supposed to."

Maya looked at him.

"You just did all three."

Jonah looked back at the board.

He had.

---

At break, Priya and Leo were arguing about whether the football had crossed the line.

Not a serious argument. The sort where both people were still eating crisps.

Maya listened for a while.

Then she said, "You're trying to settle it."

Priya stopped chewing.

"What?"

"You're both trying to get to a point where the question doesn't need deciding anymore."

Leo looked suspicious.

"Are you doing school at us?"

"No. I just noticed it."

There was a pause.

"She's right, though," said Priya.

Leo groaned.

---

After break, Mr Vale put five things on the front table.

A toy car with one wheel beside it.

A knotted shoelace.

A sheet containing the rules for a playground game.

A bowl, a spoon, and ingredients for a simple dressing.

And a hand-drawn map with a bridge marked **CLOSED**.

There were still no sentence strips.

"What are we doing?" asked Jonah.

"I don't know," said Mr Vale.

That got everyone's attention.

"You brought the stuff," said Maya.

"I know what I brought. I don't know what you're going to notice."

He pointed to the board.

"Yesterday you asked whether those were the three questions. Today I'd rather find out what that question is asking."

The instruction was almost annoyingly short:

> **Do something with one of the things. Then see whether our questions still work.**

---

Priya's group took the toy car.

The wheel had come off because a small plastic pin had slipped out. They pushed the wheel back into place, found the pin, and pressed it through the axle.

Then they rolled the car across the table.

"Repair," said Jonah.

"Too easy," said Priya.

"The wheel was the place where it had to be fixed," said Maya.

"And what remains?" asked Jonah.

Priya rolled the car back toward him.

"It can do this again."

Nobody wrote anything down for a moment.

"That's weird," Maya said.

"What is?"

"There isn't a sentence."

They all looked at the car.

The car rolled off the edge of the table.

Jonah caught it.

"Still repair," he said.

---

At the next table, Leo's group had chosen the map.

The bridge was closed, but Mr Vale gave them one extra piece of information: a footbridge had opened farther north.

They added it.

"Opening," Leo said immediately.

"But that's just the map," said Sara.

Leo frowned.

"No. The map is telling us the route is available."

"So is the opening on the paper or at the river?"

"At the river."

"But there isn't a river."

"There is in the thing the map is about."

Sara tapped the pencil against the table.

"So we're not sorting the pencil marks."

Leo looked toward the board.

"We never were."

---

The dressing caused more trouble.

Oil, lemon juice, mustard, salt.

At first the ingredients sat separately in the bowl.

Then Maya whisked them.

"Integration," said Priya.

"Maybe," said Maya. "But is it Integration because we used the word *dressing*?"

"We didn't."

"Mr Vale did. What if he'd put the things there and said nothing?"

They looked at the bowl.

Jonah said, "Maybe the words let you say what happened."

"That's not the same as causing it," said Maya.

Mr Vale was passing.

"Do you want me to answer that?"

Maya thought about it.

"No. I don't think we know the question properly yet."

Mr Vale nodded once and kept walking.

---

By lunchtime, the board had become untidy.

Under the three questions were examples:

**glasses · football argument · toy car · footbridge · dressing · shoelace · game rule**

Sara drew a line underneath them and wrote:

> **NONE OF THESE WERE SENTENCE STRIPS**

Leo added:

> **BUT THE QUESTIONS STILL WORKED**

And underneath:

> **WHY?**

That was the part everyone kept looking at.

---

After lunch, Mr Vale gave every group a large sheet with four headings.

**WE CAN ANSWER THIS NOW**

**WE COULD TEST THIS**

**KEEP THIS QUESTION**

**NOT YET**

"What's *not yet*?" asked Leo.

"A question that might be good but that we don't yet have enough to answer."

"How do we know?"

"That can be one of the things you argue about."

Maya smiled.

"So now we're sorting questions. Into piles."

"Apparently."

"We're back where we started."

---

The questions multiplied.

> **Do the patterns only work on sentences?**
>
> **Did we invent the patterns or notice them?**
>
> **Are the patterns in the words or in what happened?**
>
> **Or in how we understand what happened?**
>
> **Have we always been doing this without knowing?**
>
> **Could two people put the same thing in different places?**
>
> **Does everything have one of these patterns?**
>
> **Could something fit perfectly and still be wrong?**
>
> **Why are there three questions?**
>
> **Are there more than three?**
>
> **Why do the same kinds of answer keep coming back?**

That last question stopped them.

Jonah pointed at the six names they already knew.

"We found six answers to the first question."

Priya pointed at the three names from the second.

"And three answers to that one."

Then everyone looked at the third question.

They still did not have names for its answers.

But they had seen three shapes there too.

"Three again," said Sara.

"Maybe," said Maya.

"We saw three."

"That's not the same as knowing there are only three."

Mr Vale smiled but said nothing.

---

Near the end of the day, they returned to the toy car.

"Suppose we had never spoken about this car," said Mr Vale. "Suppose the wheel came off, Priya repaired it, and the car rolled again. Would the repair depend on us describing it?"

"No," said Leo.

"Would our *classification* depend on making the event intelligible somehow?"

That took longer.

"Yes," said Maya eventually. "Because we have to know what completion we're talking about."

"So are the patterns in the world or in our understanding?" asked Sara.

Nobody answered.

Priya walked to the four headings and placed the question under:

> **KEEP THIS QUESTION**

Maya moved it.

She put it under:

> **NOT YET**

Then she thought again and placed it exactly between the two.

Everyone laughed.

Mr Vale left it there.

---

## The point of today

The class had not proved that its three questions described the universe.

It had discovered something smaller and more interesting:

> **The questions were not merely tricks for sorting sentence strips.**

They survived changes of topic.

They survived the removal of the strips.

They could be tested against repairs, openings, rules, arguments, mixtures, routes, and ordinary events.

That gave the class evidence.

It also gave them better questions.

And a good question did not become bad merely because the answer was not ready.

---

## Four ways to treat a question

From now on, the class kept four possibilities available:

1. **Answer now** — we have enough to answer responsibly.
2. **Test it** — gather contrasts and see whether the claim survives.
3. **Keep it open** — the question is useful even without a settled answer.
4. **Not yet** — we need distinctions we have not earned yet.

This became part of the method.

Not knowing was no longer the same thing as being lost.

---

## Your turn: test it somewhere else

Choose something ordinary that was not designed for this book.

For example:

- fixing a zip;
- deciding whose turn it is;
- opening a shortcut;
- arranging players into a team;
- discovering which charger fits;
- settling how to divide something;
- combining parts into a working whole.

Ask:

> **What kind of change happened?**
>
> **What made that change enough?**
>
> **How does the achieved result remain available?**

Then ask a fourth question:

> **Did the three questions teach you something about the words, the situation, your understanding of it — or some combination you cannot yet separate?**

You do not have to settle the fourth question.

You do have to notice what evidence you have.

---

## Keep these questions

Before leaving, the class chose three questions not to solve yet:

> **Did we invent these patterns or notice them?**
>
> **Are they in the world, in language, in understanding, or somehow across those?**
>
> **How far does this way of seeing reach?**

Mr Vale drew no answers beneath them.

The empty space was deliberate.

---

## One thing we can do next

There was, however, one unfinished piece of ordinary classroom business.

The third question had kept giving them three recognizably different answers.

They already knew the difference.

They just did not yet have the short formal names.

Tomorrow they would.
