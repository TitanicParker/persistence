# Chapter 4 — Six Ways Something Can Change

*Pixels of Clarity*

[← Chapter 3](/persistence/pixels-of-clarity/chapter-03/) · **Chapter 4 of 18** · *The First Six Families*

---

The next morning, the six large cards were still pinned to the wall.

1. **A difference starts to matter.**
2. **Something becomes available.**
3. **How things bear on each other becomes important.**
4. **Something was wrong and now it has been put right.**
5. **Separate parts gain places in a whole.**
6. **Something that still needed an answer no longer does.**

The children already knew the piles.

They had argued over them.

They had moved cards between them.

They had found examples that nearly fitted two piles and had to decide which change governed the sentence.

Now the teacher placed a small card beneath the first pile.

It contained one word:

> **Differentiation**

“That's a big word for *telling things apart*,” someone said.

“Yes,” said the teacher.

“But is that all it means?”

The room went quiet.

They knew this trick by now.

---

## 1. Differentiation

The teacher put three familiar strips on the table:

> **Now we can tell them apart.**
>
> **We can see where the two routes separate.**
>
> **The mark shows which side is which.**

“What was unresolved?”

“The difference.”

“Was there definitely no difference before?”

“No.”

That mattered.

Two things can already be different without the difference being available in the way the situation requires.

Two keys may be different, but if you cannot tell which opens which door, the useful distinction is not yet settled.

Two routes may already go to different places, but if the map does not show where they separate, the relevant difference is not yet available.

So the class sharpened its description:

> **Differentiation: a difference becomes determinate enough to matter.**

The teacher underlined **becomes determinate**.

Differentiation was not simply “there are two different things.”

It was a completed change in which a previously unavailable, flattened, confused, or unusable difference became available as a difference.

A child tried it aloud.

“Before, the difference wasn't doing the job we needed. After, it is.”

Good enough for now.

---

## 2. Opening

The second formal card went up:

> **Opening**

Under it were familiar strips:

> **There is a way through now.**
>
> **We can finally continue.**
>
> **That option is available now.**

The class had called this pile *something becomes available*.

Opening was the sharper name.

> **Opening: access, reach, continuation, availability, or possibility becomes greater than it was.**

A child looked at the word *opening*.

“So it doesn't have to be an actual opening.”

“Exactly.”

A door can open.

But so can a route, an opportunity, a possibility, a continuation, a place in a game, a way of solving a problem.

The important structure is not a physical gap.

It is that something previously unavailable under the situation's constraint has become available.

Then someone asked:

“What if a broken door is repaired and that lets us through? Is that Repair or Opening?”

The teacher smiled.

“Which completion is the sentence giving you?”

They compared:

> **The broken door works properly again.**

and

> **We can get through the door now.**

Same event in the world, perhaps.

Different completed intelligibility.

The first gives the repair as complete.

The second gives the access as complete.

The children wrote in the margin:

> **Same situation does not always mean same structure.**

---

## 3. Relating

The third card was:

> **Relating**

The temporary description had been the hardest:

> **How things bear on each other becomes important.**

The teacher returned to four examples:

> **One depends on the other.**
>
> **This tells us how the two things compare.**
>
> **Their positions only make sense in relation to each other.**
>
> **What happens here changes what happens there.**

Relating did not merely mean that two things were mentioned together.

Nor did it mean that two objects happened to touch.

The relation had to become constitutive of the completed situation.

The teacher offered the sharper formulation:

> **Relating: a bearing, dependency, correspondence, ordering, reciprocity, or mutual determination becomes part of what makes the situation what it is.**

“That is definitely the longest one,” somebody said.

“For now.”

The child version remained underneath:

> **How things stand with each other becomes part of the answer.**

The class tested it.

> **The red book is beside the blue book.**

Maybe relating, if the completed point is their positional relation.

> **The red book and the blue book are heavy.**

Not merely because two books appear in the same sentence.

Relating was not counting participants.

It was identifying what kind of unresolvedness had been answered.

---

## 4. Repair

The fourth card said:

> **Repair**

This one felt familiar before it was explained.

But the class had already learned to distrust words that felt too easy.

> **The leak has been sealed.**
>
> **The damaged hinge works properly again.**
>
> **The error has been corrected.**

“What makes these Repair rather than just Change?”

“There was something wrong.”

“Wrong according to what?”

“The way it was supposed to work.”

That was the key.

Repair needs an operative criterion: some way the thing is meant, required, expected, or organized to be.

> **Repair: something failing relative to an operative criterion is changed so that the fault or misfit no longer governs the result.**

The easier version stayed beside it:

> **Something that was wrong is put right enough.**

“Enough?”

“Yes,” said the teacher.

“A repaired bicycle does not have to become the best bicycle in the world. The relevant fault has to stop governing the result.”

That word—**enough**—would become much more important later.

---

## 5. Integration

The fifth card read:

> **Integration**

The children remembered the paper clips.

Simply putting things beside one another was not enough.

Simply joining them physically was not necessarily enough either.

The examples had a stronger form:

> **The players now work as one team.**
>
> **The separate jobs fit into one plan.**
>
> **The pieces now work as one.**

The teacher wrote:

> **Integration: separate parts acquire determinate roles through organization into a whole.**

Then underneath:

> **The parts gain their places in one organized thing.**

A pile of bricks is many bricks together.

A wall is different.

Eleven footballers standing in the same field are a group of people.

Eleven players whose positions and actions have become organized into a functioning side exhibit something more.

Integration was not mere togetherness.

The whole had to matter to what the parts now were or could do.

---

## 6. Settlement

The final card said:

> **Settlement**

The class returned to the sentence one child had invented the day before:

> **There was still something that had to be answered, and now there isn't.**

The teacher said they were keeping it.

The formal version was:

> **Settlement: a live demand for further uptake, choice, explanation, completion, adjudication, or continuation is discharged.**

Nobody preferred it.

But they could see why it was more exact.

Settlement was not merely that time had passed.

It was not merely that people stopped talking.

It was not merely that somebody walked away.

Something had been live—something still calling for an answer, decision, completion, response, or continuation—and that demand no longer governed what remained.

> **That settles it.**
>
> **We have decided who goes first.**
>
> **Nothing more is owed between us.**
>
> **That question has been answered.**

Different topics.

Same broad transformation family.

---

## The wall has names now

The teacher replaced the six temporary cards with six larger ones.

| What the class first noticed | Formal name |
| --- | --- |
| A difference becomes usable | **Differentiation** |
| Something becomes available | **Opening** |
| How things stand with each other becomes part of the answer | **Relating** |
| Something wrong is put right enough | **Repair** |
| Separate parts gain places in a whole | **Integration** |
| Something still demanding an answer stops demanding one | **Settlement** |

“These are definitions?” asked a child.

“They are our working handles.”

“Are they perfect?”

“No.”

“Then why use them?”

“Because now we can test them more precisely.”

The words had not replaced the examples.

The examples gave the words their grip.

---

## The six-way test

The teacher handed out a fresh set.

For each sentence, the children had to choose one of the six names and give a reason.

> **The labels now show which cable is which.**

**Differentiation** — the relevant difference has become determinate and usable.

> **There is finally a route around the flooded road.**

**Opening** — continuation that was unavailable has become available.

> **The price changes with the weight.**

**Relating** — a dependency between the values is constitutive of the answer.

> **The loose connection has been corrected.**

**Repair** — a misfit relative to proper functioning no longer governs the result.

> **The separate sections now form one working machine.**

**Integration** — the parts have acquired roles within an organized whole.

> **We have agreed on the final score.**

**Settlement** — the live demand for further determination has been discharged.

Then the teacher removed the explanations.

New cards.

No hints.

Then stranger examples.

Then examples where two transformations seemed nearby.

The children were no longer matching words.

They were testing boundaries.

---

## The neighbour challenge

The teacher wrote two sentences:

> **The broken gate works again.**
>
> **We can get into the field again.**

“Same story?”

“Could be.”

“Same transformation?”

“No.”

The first completes **Repair**.

The second completes **Opening**.

Then:

> **We can now tell the twins apart.**
>
> **We now know how the twins are related.**

**Differentiation** versus **Relating**.

Then:

> **Everyone now has a role in the group.**
>
> **Everyone has agreed what their role will be.**

The first leans toward **Integration**: roles constituted through an organized whole.

The second leans toward **Settlement**: a live matter of agreement has been discharged.

“Why do you keep giving us pairs that are almost the same?”

“Because easy examples tell me whether you remember the names.”

“And these?”

“These tell me whether you can see the difference.”

---

## One question is doing a lot of work

By now, the first structural question had become almost automatic:

> **What kind of change happened?**

When necessary, they expanded it:

> **What was unresolved before, and what kind of unresolvedness has now been answered?**

The six answers were:

**Differentiation** — a relevant difference becomes determinate.

**Opening** — relevant availability becomes greater.

**Relating** — a constitutive bearing or dependency becomes determinate.

**Repair** — a fault or misfit stops governing the result.

**Integration** — separate parts acquire roles in an organized whole.

**Settlement** — a live demand for further resolution is discharged.

Six ways for the first dial to turn.

Except nobody had called it a dial yet.

---

## Then the piles broke again

The class might have stopped there.

Six families was already a powerful way to sort ordinary sentences.

But one child had been rearranging the Repair pile.

She put three cards side by side:

> **That part is fixed now.**
>
> **The faults across the whole system have been put right.**
>
> **The old fault no longer controls what happens.**

“They're all Repair,” she said.

“Yes.”

“But they don't finish the same way.”

The teacher said nothing.

The child pointed to the first.

“This one seems to finish *there*.”

Then the second.

“This one only seems finished because lots of parts are right together.”

Then the third.

“And this one is strange. It seems finished because the old problem isn't in charge anymore.”

The room became quiet again.

They had solved the first sorting problem.

And solving it had exposed another one.

The six names could tell them **what kind of change happened**.

But they could not yet tell them:

> **What made that change count as complete?**

That would require a second question.
