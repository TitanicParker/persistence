# Chapter 1 — The Bag of Sentences

*Pixels of Clarity*

[← Book home](/persistence/pixels-of-clarity/) · **Chapter 1 of 18** · *The Pile*

---

The teacher came into the room carrying a bag.

It was not a particularly interesting bag.

It was not locked. It did not glow. There was no secret map inside it, no treasure, no strange machine, and no note saying **DO NOT OPEN**.

It was just a bag.

But it was full of sentences.

Not pages. Not stories. Sentences.

Each sentence had been printed on its own strip of paper.

The teacher tipped the bag over.

Hundreds of white strips slid across the table and onto the floor.

Some landed face up. Some landed face down. Some crossed over each other. One disappeared under a chair.

For a few seconds, everybody looked at the mess.

Then the teacher said:

> “First, turn them all the right way up.”

That seemed easy enough.

So everybody began.

---

## A strange collection

Once the strips were face up, people started reading them.

> **“That settles it.”**

> **“It all hangs together.”**

> **“There is a way through now.”**

> **“That part is fixed.”**

> **“Now we can tell them apart.”**

> **“We’re square now.”**

> **“The pieces work together.”**

> **“That no longer holds us back.”**

> **“This tells us where we stand.”**

> **“The whole thing works now.”**

They were ordinary sentences.

Nobody needed a dictionary to understand them.

But they were a peculiar collection.

One child picked up **“That part is fixed.”**

Another found **“The whole thing works now.”**

“Those go together,” she said.

“Why?” asked the teacher.

“Because they’re both about fixing things.”

The teacher nodded, but did not say whether she was right.

Across the table, somebody had put **“That settles it”** beside **“We’re square now.”**

“Those go together too.”

“Why?”

“Because something is finished.”

Someone else disagreed.

“Lots of these are finished. **‘That part is fixed’** is finished too.”

That was true.

The first little problem had appeared.

---

## Your first job

The teacher did not explain the sentences.

Instead, she pushed a small group of strips into the middle of the table.

> **That part is fixed now.**
>
> **There is a way through now.**
>
> **Now we can tell them apart.**
>
> **It all fits together.**
>
> **That settles the matter.**
>
> **This depends on that.**
>
> **The whole thing works now.**
>
> **We can leave it there.**
>
> **The pieces have their places now.**
>
> **That rule doesn’t stop us anymore.**
>
> **These two are different after all.**
>
> **One tells us where the other stands.**

“Arrange them,” she said.

“How?”

“However you think makes sense.”

“How many groups?”

“I don’t know.”

“Is there a right answer?”

The teacher smiled.

“There are better and worse reasons.”

That was not quite the answer anyone wanted.

But it was enough to begin.

---

## What counts as a group?

At first, almost any arrangement seemed possible.

You could put all the short sentences together.

You could put sentences containing the word *now* together.

You could put sentences that sounded cheerful in one pile and serious ones in another.

You could even sort them by how long the strips of paper were.

Those would all be real arrangements.

But something felt wrong with them.

The sentences had not been collected because some were short and some were long.

The word *now* did not seem to be the secret either.

There seemed to be something happening **inside what the sentences meant**.

The class tried again.

One group began with:

> **Now we can tell them apart.**
>
> **These two are different after all.**

Another began with:

> **That part is fixed now.**
>
> **The whole thing works now.**

Another had:

> **That settles the matter.**
>
> **We can leave it there.**

And another:

> **It all fits together.**
>
> **The pieces have their places now.**

The piles were still imperfect.

But something had changed.

The class was no longer sorting the **paper**.

They were beginning to sort **what was happening**.

---

## The teacher’s annoying question

Every time somebody moved a strip, the teacher asked the same question.

> **“What made you put those together?”**

After a while, it became annoying.

“They just belong together.”

“What makes them belong together?”

“They mean nearly the same thing.”

“What is the same?”

“They’re both about something being sorted out.”

“What got sorted out?”

Sometimes a child had an answer immediately.

Sometimes everybody stared at the sentences.

Sometimes two people had different answers.

And sometimes a sentence that looked as though it obviously belonged in one pile suddenly looked better in another.

The piles kept changing.

That turned out to be important.

The aim was not to make a neat table as quickly as possible.

The aim was to find **good reasons** for the table.

---

## Try it without us

Before you read further, take these six sentences:

1. **Now I know which key opens it.**
2. **The broken wheel turns properly again.**
3. **We have agreed what happens next.**
4. **The two pieces now work as one.**
5. **The path is open again.**
6. **This mark tells us which side is which.**

Make some groups.

There is no need to use a special number of groups.

There is no need to know any special words.

Just make an arrangement you can defend.

Then ask yourself:

> **What made me put those together?**

If somebody else is with you, let them rearrange the same six strips.

Do not immediately decide which arrangement is correct.

Instead ask:

> **What did you notice that I didn’t?**

That question will matter later.

---

## A warning about words

One group found two sentences containing the word *together* and placed them beside each other.

That seemed sensible.

Then somebody found a third sentence:

> **We decided together.**

They added it to the pile.

But now the pile felt strange.

**“The pieces fit together”** and **“We decided together”** both contain the same word, but the word is doing very different work.

The teacher wrote something on the board:

> **Same word does not always mean same structure.**

Then underneath:

> **Different words can hide the same structure.**

Nobody was asked to memorize this.

It was simply something the class had already discovered by making a bad pile.

---

## The pile gets harder

The teacher added more strips.

Some were easy.

Some seemed to belong in two places.

Some made everybody disagree.

One child wanted to make a pile called **FIXED**.

Another wanted **FINISHED**.

Another wanted **CONNECTED**.

Someone else said that **“It all hangs together”** sounded both connected *and* finished.

“So where does it go?”

Nobody knew yet.

The teacher did not rescue them.

Instead she asked:

> “What question would help us decide?”

That was different.

Until then, the class had been trying to find answers.

Now they had to find a **question**.

---

## The first discovery

By the end of the lesson, the floor was still covered in paper.

There was no finished system.

There were no official names on the wall.

Nobody had been told that there were a certain number of kinds.

But the room was not the same as it had been when the bag was emptied.

At the beginning, there had only been sentences.

Now there were sentences **and comparisons**.

There were disagreements.

There were reasons.

There were sentences that looked similar but behaved differently.

And there were sentences that used completely different words but somehow seemed to have the same shape.

The class had discovered something small but powerful:

> **A pile is not just a pile. A pile is a claim about what is the same.**

And if you make that claim, somebody can ask:

> **What made you put those together?**

That is where our investigation begins.

---

## Take the question home

You do not need printed cards for this part.

Listen for six ordinary sentences today.

They might come from:

- something somebody says at home;
- a game;
- a story;
- a lesson;
- something going wrong and being fixed;
- somebody making a decision;
- people working something out.

Write the six sentences down as closely as you can remember them.

Then try making two or three piles.

For every pile, finish this sentence:

> **I put these together because…**

Do not worry about being right.

For now, the important thing is learning to **notice what you are noticing**.

**Are we sorting by what the sentence is about—or by what happened inside it?**
