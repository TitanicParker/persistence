# Chapter 2 — Piles Are Arguments

*Pixels of Clarity*

[← Chapter 1](/persistence/pixels-of-clarity/chapter-01/) · **Chapter 2 of 18** · *The Pile*

---

The next morning, the piles were still there.

Not exactly as they had been left. Someone had opened a window, and three strips had blown under a cupboard. A chair leg was standing on **“That settles the matter.”**

But most of yesterday’s work had survived.

The teacher looked at it.

“So,” she said, “are we finished?”

Nobody thought so.

One group had seven piles. Another had five. Two children had made a special pile called **THINGS WORKING**, while somebody else had put most of those same sentences into **THINGS FIXED**.

Then there was the sentence nobody could agree about:

> **It all hangs together.**

It had moved four times.

The teacher picked it up.

“Yesterday,” she said, “we learned that making a pile is making a claim.”

She put the strip back down.

“Today we find out what happens when two claims disagree.”

---

## The same six sentences, two different maps

The teacher chose six strips.

> **The broken wheel turns properly again.**
>
> **We have agreed what happens next.**
>
> **The two pieces now work as one.**
>
> **The path is open again.**
>
> **Now I know which key opens it.**
>
> **This mark tells us which side is which.**

She gave copies to two tables.

“Make groups,” she said. “But this time, write the reason for every group.”

The first table worked quickly.

They made three piles.

### Table A

**Things about objects**

- The broken wheel turns properly again.
- The two pieces now work as one.
- Now I know which key opens it.

**Things about people**

- We have agreed what happens next.

**Things about places or directions**

- The path is open again.
- This mark tells us which side is which.

“That works,” somebody said.

And it did.

The piles were not nonsense. The reasons were clear. You could explain every card.

At the second table, the children had done something quite different.

### Table B

**Something that was not working now works**

- The broken wheel turns properly again.

**Something that was unavailable is now available**

- The path is open again.

**Something uncertain has become decided or distinguishable**

- We have agreed what happens next.
- Now I know which key opens it.
- This mark tells us which side is which.

**Separate things now make one working arrangement**

- The two pieces now work as one.

“That works too,” somebody said.

And it did.

Now there was a problem.

---

## Can two different sorts both be right?

The teacher drew two boxes on the board.

In the first she wrote:

> **WHAT IS THE SENTENCE ABOUT?**

In the second:

> **WHAT HAPPENED?**

“Table A,” she said, “what were you mostly looking at?”

The children looked at their piles.

“Objects. People. Places.”

“So you were looking at what the sentences were about.”

They nodded.

“Table B?”

“We were looking at what changed.”

The teacher nodded.

Then she asked a question that sounded almost unfair:

> **Which sort is better?**

Table A immediately defended itself.

“Ours is easier.”

“It’s really clear.”

“You can tell where things go.”

Table B objected.

“But a sentence can be about a wheel and another about a friendship, and the same kind of thing could happen in both.”

“Like what?”

“Something could be fixed.”

The room went quiet for a moment.

That was useful.

---

## A bicycle and a friendship

The teacher wrote two new sentences.

> **The bicycle is fixed now.**

and

> **They fixed their friendship.**

“Same topic?” she asked.

“No.”

“Same objects?”

“No.”

“Same people?”

“No.”

“Anything alike?”

A child said, “Something was wrong and now it isn’t.”

The teacher underlined that sentence.

Not *bicycle*.

Not *friendship*.

> **Something was wrong and now it isn’t.**

Then she tried another pair.

> **The gate is open now.**

> **There is now a way to ask questions.**

One was about a gate. The other might be about a classroom, a website, a meeting, or a person becoming willing to listen.

Yet somebody said:

“In both of them, something you couldn’t get through before is available now.”

The teacher underlined that too.

The class was beginning to see why Table B mattered.

---

## Topic is useful — but it is not our quarry

There was nothing wrong with sorting by topic.

Libraries do it.

Shops do it.

Websites do it.

Your kitchen probably does it. Plates go with plates. Spices go with spices. Cleaning things do not usually go in the biscuit tin.

Topic is extremely useful when topic is what you need.

But the teacher had collected these sentences for a different reason.

She was not trying to discover whether the world contained bicycles, friendships, gates, paths, agreements, wheels, teams, recipes, keys, rules, or arguments.

She was asking whether very different situations could have the **same kind of intelligible shape**.

Nobody used those exact words yet.

The children said things like:

> “The same sort of thing happened.”

or

> “They got to the same kind of ending.”

or

> “They’re different stories, but they do the same move.”

That last one stayed on the board.

> **Different stories can do the same move.**

---

## The sentence that fools you

The teacher now put down three strips.

> **The red team won.**
>
> **The red wire is the live one.**
>
> **Now we can tell the red one from the orange one.**

“What pile?”

“Red things,” somebody said immediately.

Everybody laughed.

It was exactly the sort of pile they had learned to distrust.

But the teacher did not throw it away.

“Suppose I am decorating a poster about the colour red. Is it a good pile?”

“Yes.”

“Suppose I am trying to discover what kind of change each sentence reports. Is it a good pile?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Because the word *red* was now doing all the work.

The children had learned an important trick:

> **A grouping can be perfectly sensible and still be wrong for the question you are asking.**

That meant there was more to sorting than finding similarities.

You had to know **which similarities mattered**.

---

## Piles are arguments

The teacher wrote yesterday’s discovery on the board:

> **A pile is a claim about what is the same.**

Then she added one word.

> **A pile is an argument about what is the same.**

“An argument?” someone said. “Like a fight?”

“Not that kind.”

She explained that an argument can mean a reasoned claim: *I think these belong together, and here is why.*

So if you put two sentences in the same pile, your pile is quietly saying:

> **For the purpose of this investigation, these two are alike in an important way.**

And if someone moves one of them, they are saying:

> **I think the difference matters more than the similarity you noticed.**

Suddenly disagreement was not a nuisance.

It was information.

When two people disagreed about a card, that card might be exactly where the interesting distinction was hiding.

---

## The troublesome card

The teacher brought back yesterday’s troublemaker.

> **It all hangs together.**

“Tell me where it goes.”

A child put it with:

> **The two pieces now work as one.**

“Why?”

“Because things are together.”

Another child shook her head.

“That’s just the word again.”

“So what is happening?” asked the teacher.

There was a pause.

“The parts depend on each other?”

“Maybe.”

“Or the whole makes sense because the pieces fit?”

“Maybe.”

“Is that the same as two pieces starting to work as one?”

Nobody was sure.

Good.

The teacher put a small star beside the card.

“What does the star mean?”

“It means we don’t know?”

“Better. It means **this card can teach us something if we compare it carefully**.”

They made a new rule:

> **Do not hide the difficult cards. Mark them.**

A difficult card was not a failure of the game.

It was often the beginning of the next discovery.

---

## The smallest difference

The teacher now tried a different game.

She placed two sentences side by side.

> **That part is fixed now.**

> **Fixing that part makes the machine work again.**

“Same or different?”

“Same. Both are fixing.”

“Then why do I need two sentences?”

The class read them again.

One child pointed to the second.

“This one says what the fixing lets happen.”

The teacher nodded.

“So they are close.”

“Yes.”

“But not identical.”

“No.”

She drew a tiny gap between the strips.

“What is the smallest difference you can see?”

The class had been asking a large question:

> **What is the same?**

Now they had another tool:

> **What is the smallest difference?**

That question was surprisingly powerful.

Big differences are easy. A bicycle is not a friendship. A path is not an agreement.

But small structural differences can tell you where one pile ends and another begins.

---

## Your turn: two sorts

Here are eight sentences.

1. **The crack has been repaired.**
2. **We finally know which answer is correct.**
3. **The doors are open to everyone now.**
4. **The four musicians now play as one group.**
5. **The disagreement is settled.**
6. **This measurement tells us how the two sizes compare.**
7. **Removing the blockage lets the water flow again.**
8. **We can now see the difference between the two tracks.**

First, sort them by **topic**. Make any topic piles you like.

Then mix them up and sort them again by **what happened**.

For the second sort, do not use a pile name that is merely an object, person, place, or repeated word.

For every pile, complete:

> **These belong together because…**

Then choose the two sentences in your whole arrangement that seem most alike and ask:

> **What is the smallest difference between them?**

You do not need the answer the book would give.

You need an answer you can defend.

---

## A new kind of noticing

By lunchtime, the class had fewer neat piles than before.

That might sound like failure.

It was not.

Their first piles had been easy because the rules were loose. Put all the sentences about people here. Put all the ones containing *now* there. Put all the sentences that sound finished in another pile.

Now the class was demanding more from itself.

A pile had to survive questions.

Why these two?

What happened in both?

Are you grouping the topic or the move?

Does the same word fool you?

Can different words hide the same structure?

What is the smallest difference between these neighbours?

The children were not merely organizing sentences anymore.

They were learning to **compare reasons for organizing them**.

And that meant they were ready for the first question that would actually begin cutting the huge pile into a stable structure.

The teacher gathered six carefully chosen groups of cards.

She did not give them names.

She did not say how many boxes there would eventually be.

She only wrote one question on the board:

> **What kind of change happened?**

Then, underneath it, in smaller writing:

> **What kind of unresolvedness changed?**

“That,” she said, “is tomorrow’s question.”

---

## Take it home: catch the move

Find three pairs of sentences from ordinary life.

For each pair, make the topics as different as you can while keeping the **kind of thing that happened** as similar as you can.

For example, you might begin with:

> **The toy is fixed.**

and try to find a completely different situation in which something that was wrong has been put right.

Or begin with:

> **The way is open now.**

and find a situation with no road, gate, or door in it that still seems to make the same move.

For each pair write:

> **Different stories:** …
>
> **Same move:** …

If the second line is difficult to write, keep the pair. Difficult pairs are useful.

We began with a bag of sentences.
