# Chapter 16 — Clear Does Not Mean True

*Pixels of Clarity*

[← Chapter 15](/persistence/pixels-of-clarity/chapter-15/) · **Chapter 16 of 18** · *The Map Is Not a Truth Machine*

---

Sara's sentence was still on the board.

> **The treasure is buried under the oak tree.**

Underneath it, Maya's question:

> **CAN SOMETHING BE PERFECTLY INTELLIGIBLE AND STILL BE FALSE?**

Leo had written one word beneath that.

> **YES**

Mr Vale looked at it.

"Lesson finished, then."

Nobody moved.

"That was easy," said Leo.

Priya pointed at the sentence.

"We know the answer. I don't think we know what it does to the map."

That was different.

---

## The oak tree

There really was an oak tree at the edge of the playground.

There was not, as far as anyone knew, treasure beneath it.

Mr Vale put a small metal tin on his desk.

"Pretend this is the treasure."

He gave it to Jonah.

Jonah went outside.

Five minutes later he returned without the tin.

"The treasure is buried under the oak tree," he said.

The class knew exactly what he was claiming.

They could ask what had become determinate.

They could ask what made the determination sufficient.

They could ask how the result remained available.

Whatever structural analysis they finally defended, the claim was clear enough to investigate.

"Now find out whether he's right," said Mr Vale.

They went outside.

Maya dug carefully where Jonah indicated.

Nothing.

They widened the hole.

Nothing.

Jonah started laughing.

"I put it under the bench."

Leo turned immediately toward the classroom.

"So the coordinate was wrong."

Priya stopped walking.

"Why?"

"Because the sentence was false."

"Which dial changed?"

Leo opened his mouth.

Then closed it.

---

## Which dial changes when a claim is false?

Back inside, Maya drew the three dials.

> **What kind of change happened?**
>
> **What made that change enough?**
>
> **How does the achieved result remain available?**

Then she wrote:

> **FALSE**

"Which answer changes just because we found out Jonah lied?"

Nobody could find one.

The claim had not become harder to understand when the shovel found empty soil.

What Jonah had made intelligible remained what Jonah had made intelligible.

What changed was their judgment about whether his claim matched the world.

Sara drew a line down the board.

On one side:

> **WHAT DOES THIS MAKE INTELLIGIBLE?**

On the other:

> **IS IT TRUE?**

"Different questions," she said.

It was Chapter 2 all over again.

A perfectly sensible operation could still be the wrong operation for the question you were trying to answer.

---

## Two boxes

Mr Vale put two closed cardboard boxes on the table.

He held up a red token and a blue token.

Then he sent the class into the corridor while he arranged them.

When they returned, two cards were waiting.

> **The red token is inside Box A.**

> **The blue token is inside Box B.**

"Do you understand both claims?" he asked.

Yes.

"Is one sentence structurally clearer than the other?"

No.

"Can the three questions tell us which token is actually where?"

They tried.

Then laughed.

"No," said Leo. "We have to open the boxes."

They did.

The red token was in Box A.

The blue token was also in Box A.

One claim was true.

One was false.

Yet both had been equally understandable before the lids came off.

Maya wrote:

> **UNDERSTANDABLE ENOUGH TO TEST ≠ ALREADY TRUE**

---

## Clear enough to be wrong

Jonah looked puzzled.

"So being clear is what lets something be properly wrong?"

"Sometimes," said Priya.

That sounded strange until they tried the opposite.

Leo invented a sentence:

> **Flarn the seven beneath because maybe.**

"False," he announced.

"What does it claim?" asked Maya.

"I don't know."

"Then what exactly are you calling false?"

He had no answer.

Before a claim could be tested, there had to be enough determination to know what was at stake.

Structural intelligibility did not make a claim true.

It could make a claim **available for judgment**.

That was a very different power.

---

## The map is not a gold star

The class had become good at finding coordinates.

That success had quietly made coordinates feel approving.

As though locating something cleanly meant it had passed.

Mr Vale wrote:

> **A COORDINATE IS NOT A GOLD STAR.**

Then the class made a list.

A confident structural analysis did **not** prove:

- that a claim was true;
- that a speaker was honest;
- that an explanation was scientifically correct;
- that a repair really worked in the world;
- that a decision was wise;
- that an agreement was fair;
- that a rule was good.

The map answered the questions it was built to answer.

It did not answer every question worth asking.

---

## A matter can be settled wrongly

Maya wrote another sentence.

> **The class decided that Leo broke the model.**

"I didn't," said Leo immediately.

"There isn't even a model," said Sara.

"Pretend there was."

They did.

Suppose the class argued for twenty minutes.

Suppose everyone finally agreed Leo had broken it.

Suppose the matter was settled.

Could the settlement be structurally intelligible?

Yes.

Could the decision still be wrong?

Yes.

Could everyone agree and still be unfair to Leo?

Yes.

The room became quieter.

Settlement was not truth.

Settlement was not justice.

Agreement was not proof.

Priya wrote beneath the example:

> **A matter can become settled without being settled rightly.**

That sentence stayed on the board.

---

## A rule can be clear and terrible

Leo, enjoying his role as the victim of imaginary injustice, proposed a rule:

> **Only children wearing blue may use the playground.**

Everyone understood it.

Nobody thought it was a good rule.

"But it's very clear," said Sara.

Exactly.

A rule could be intelligible and unfair.

A decision could be intelligible and mistaken.

A story could be intelligible and fictional.

A prediction could be intelligible and fail.

A lie could be intelligible precisely because somebody wanted another person to understand the false thing being claimed.

The grammar was not a morality machine either.

---

## So what does truth need?

"Then how do we find out what's true?" asked Leo.

Mr Vale looked at the three dials.

"Do those answer that?"

"No."

"Then don't make them."

They listed ordinary ways people find things out:

looking,

measuring,

checking records,

remembering,

comparing evidence,

asking reliable witnesses,

testing,

repeating an experiment,

checking a source,

seeing what actually happens.

Different claims required different kinds of checking.

They did not need a complete theory of truth to understand the boundary.

For truth, you had to do whatever the claim required to find out whether things really were as claimed.

The map could help make the claim clear enough to test.

It could not perform the test for you.

---

## The questions have jobs

Sara returned to the line she had drawn on the board.

On the left:

> **What does this make intelligible?**

On the right:

> **Is it true?**

Then Maya added:

> **Is it fair?**

Jonah added:

> **Is it good?**

Priya added:

> **What evidence do we have?**

Leo added:

> **Should we believe the person saying it?**

The board was becoming crowded.

"So there are loads of maps," he said.

"Maybe," said Maya. "Or loads of questions."

Nobody tried to settle that.

The important thing was simpler.

> **STRUCTURE MAKES JUDGMENT POSSIBLE. IT DOES NOT REPLACE JUDGMENT.**

---

## The old mistake returns

Mr Vale picked up the false token card.

> **The blue token is inside Box B.**

"What did we do wrong when we expected the three dials to tell us whether this was true?"

Priya answered first.

"We asked a tool to answer a question it wasn't built to answer."

Leo pointed at her.

"That's the piles!"

Back at the beginning, a pile could be perfectly sensible and still be wrong **for the question being asked**.

Now the same discipline had returned at a much larger scale.

A structural analysis could be perfectly good and still not answer:

> **Is this true?**

The children had not merely learned another warning.

They had recognized an old principle in a new place.

---

## Your turn: two judgments

For each statement, keep two questions separate.

### A

> **The library closes at four o'clock.**

1. Can you understand what is being claimed well enough to investigate it?
2. What would you need to check to find out whether it is true today?

### B

> **Everyone agreed that Sam should pay for the broken window.**

1. What has become settled in the statement?
2. Does the settlement prove Sam broke the window or that making Sam pay is fair?

### C

> **The repaired bridge is safe to cross.**

1. What completed intelligibility is being presented?
2. What evidence would you want before walking across?

Then ask:

> **Which questions belong to structural intelligibility, and which require another kind of judgment?**

---

## Something the map cannot prove about itself

Near the end of the lesson, Jonah was staring at the fifty-four-box board.

"There's another problem."

"Good," said Priya.

"We keep using examples made for us."

"Not all of them."

"No. But loads of them. The sentences in the Goldmine were made to help the boxes speak. The neighbour examples were made to show differences. Even today's boxes were set up by Mr Vale."

Maya understood before he finished.

"So maybe we're good at a game whose examples were designed for the game."

Nobody liked that possibility.

Leo looked at Mr Vale.

"Can the map prove that it works outside the examples?"

Mr Vale shook his head.

"What do you think?"

Leo looked toward the classroom door.

"We'd have to go outside."
